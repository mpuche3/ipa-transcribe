# Reengineering the Corporation: Manufacturing, Logistics and the Revolution It Named

Oct 31, 2026 · @Miguel Puche

## Introduction: A Book That Named an Era

When Michael Hammer and James Champy published Reengineering the Corporation: A Manifesto for Business Revolution in 1993, they did something that few management books ever achieve. They did not simply describe a technique; they gave a name to a mood that was already spreading through boardrooms in the United States and Europe. Western industry had spent more than a decade watching Japanese manufacturers take market share in automobiles, consumer electronics, machine tools and semiconductors. Companies had tried quality circles, total quality management, automation and restructuring, and many of them felt that these efforts were producing modest gains while the gap with the best competitors stayed wide. Hammer and Champy told them, in a confident and almost evangelical tone, that the problem was not that their people were lazy or their machines outdated. The problem was that the very way work was organized had been designed for a world that no longer existed.

The two authors came from different but complementary backgrounds. Michael Hammer was a former professor of computer science at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology who had become a consultant and speaker. He had already launched the core idea in a famous 1990 article in the Harvard Business Review titled "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate." The provocative subtitle captured his central argument: companies were spending fortunes on information technology to speed up old procedures, when they should instead have been using technology to eliminate those procedures entirely. James Champy was chairman of CSC Index, a consulting firm that had been working on similar ideas with large clients and that would become, for a few years, one of the most visible consultancies in the world precisely because of reengineering. Together they turned a provocative article into a full manifesto, with a definition, a vocabulary, a set of principles, a method and a long list of case studies.

The book became a phenomenon. It sold millions of copies, was translated into many languages, stayed on bestseller lists for well over a year and was later included in lists of the most influential business books of the twentieth century. Within a few years, "reengineering" had become one of the most used words in corporate life, and also one of the most feared. It was attached to enormous programs in manufacturing companies, banks, insurers, utilities, telecommunications operators and governments. It fueled the growth of the consulting industry and the market for enterprise software. It also became associated, fairly or not, with the wave of layoffs and "downsizing" that marked the early and middle 1990s.

This essay examines the book from the point of view of operations: manufacturing, production, logistics, procurement, order fulfillment and product development. Although many of the most famous examples in the book come from financial services and administrative work, the intellectual foundation of reengineering is deeply industrial. The authors begin with Adam Smith's pin factory, argue against the legacy of Henry Ford and Alfred Sloan, and propose a new way of seeing the enterprise as a set of end to end processes that flow across functional departments in exactly the way material flows through a factory. The essay therefore follows three threads. The first is the set of concepts the book introduced or popularized and how they apply to operations. The second is the story of the book itself, its origins and its reception. The third is its impact on industry and society, including the backlash it provoked and the legacy it left in supply chain management, enterprise systems, lean thinking and today's discussions of digital transformation.

## The Old Industrial Paradigm: Smith, Ford and Sloan

Hammer and Champy built their argument on a historical reading of how modern corporations came to be organized. Their starting point is Adam Smith's description, in The Wealth of Nations (1776), of a pin factory in which the manufacture of a simple pin was divided into about eighteen distinct operations. One worker drew out the wire, another straightened it, a third cut it, a fourth pointed it, a fifth ground the top to receive the head, and so on. Smith observed that ten workers organized in this way could produce tens of thousands of pins a day, whereas each of them working alone might struggle to make twenty. The division of labor, with its specialization, repetition and gains in dexterity, was in Smith's view the great engine of productivity. For the authors, this insight was the foundational principle of the industrial age, and for two centuries it was spectacularly successful.

The second figure in their story is Henry Ford. Ford took the division of labor to a new level by combining it with interchangeable parts and the moving assembly line. At Highland Park, beginning around 1913, the time needed to assemble a Model T chassis fell from more than twelve hours to roughly an hour and a half. Each worker performed a small, repetitive task while the work moved past. Ford's insight was to bring the work to the worker rather than the worker to the work, and to design jobs so simple that almost anyone could be trained to do them in a short time. The result was mass production: high volume, low cost and standardized products. Hammer and Champy point out, however, that Ford's system created a new problem. If the work was broken into very small pieces, someone had to coordinate the pieces, check their quality and plan their flow. This produced a large layer of managers, supervisors, inspectors, planners and clerks whose job was not to make anything but to glue the fragments back together.

The third figure is Alfred Sloan, who led General Motors from the 1920s and created the model of the decentralized, multidivisional corporation. Sloan applied the logic of the division of labor to management itself. Divisions were organized around products or markets, and within them work was arranged by function: engineering, manufacturing, purchasing, sales, finance. Central staffs measured performance through financial controls and allocated capital. This structure allowed GM to grow into the largest industrial company in the world, and it became the template for large corporations everywhere, including companies that had nothing to do with automobiles.

The authors argue that the combination of Smith's fragmented tasks, Ford's mass production and Sloan's functional hierarchy produced what they regarded as the typical modern corporation: tall pyramids, narrow jobs, specialist departments and a fixation on controlling work rather than performing it. In a world of growing demand, stable markets and limited competition, this model worked well. Its strengths were scale, predictability and control. Its weaknesses were hidden as long as customers accepted what they were offered. In the stable decades after the Second World War, when demand in the United States often exceeded supply, a company could afford to be slow, inflexible and expensive in its coordination costs because customers had few alternatives.

From an operations perspective, the central consequence of this paradigm was fragmentation. An order from a customer did not flow smoothly through the company. It passed from sales to credit checking, from credit checking to order entry, from order entry to production planning, from planning to the factory, from the factory to the warehouse, from the warehouse to shipping and from shipping to billing. At every handoff the work waited in a queue, information was copied, errors were introduced and someone had to check what the previous department had done. Each department optimized its own performance, measured by its own metrics, and nobody was responsible for the whole. The customer, who cared only about getting the right product at the right time and price, saw a company that was slow, error prone and impossible to talk to. Hammer and Champy famously describe this as a situation in which nobody in the company was actually responsible for the result the customer wanted.

This diagnosis was not new in itself. Operations researchers, systems theorists and quality experts such as W. Edwards Deming had long criticized departmental silos and suboptimization. What was distinctive in the book was the claim that the problem could not be solved by improving each fragment. The fragmentation itself was the problem, and only a redesign of the entire flow of work could remove it.

## The Three Cs: Customers, Competition and Change

Why, according to Hammer and Champy, had the old model stopped working? Their answer was condensed into a simple formula: three forces, which they called the three Cs, were pushing companies into unfamiliar territory. These were customers, competition and change. The formula is easy to remember and was quoted endlessly in the years that followed.

The first force was the customer. In the era of mass production, sellers held the upper hand. Customers bought standard products in large markets and had limited information and limited choice. By the 1980s, the authors argued, the balance had shifted. Customers had more options, more information and higher expectations, and they wanted products and services tailored to their own needs. The mass market had broken into segments and, increasingly, into individual customers. Industrial buyers, too, had become more demanding. An automotive manufacturer working with just in time deliveries required its suppliers to ship small quantities, frequently, with near perfect quality. A retailer wanted its suppliers to replenish shelves based on actual sales rather than forecasts. For a manufacturer, this meant that the ability to deliver variety, speed and reliability was becoming as important as unit cost.

The second force was competition. In the past, the authors said, a company that offered an acceptable product at an acceptable price could survive. Now, competition had intensified and globalized. Japanese companies in particular had shown that it was possible to offer higher quality, lower cost and faster product development at the same time, breaking the traditional assumption that these goals were in conflict. Niche competitors attacked specific segments. Deregulation opened telecommunications, airlines, trucking and financial services. New technologies allowed start ups to enter markets that had been protected by scale. In this environment, the best performer in the world set the standard, and any company that fell behind that standard on cost, quality, service or speed would lose customers. Benchmarking, which became popular in the same period, made the comparison explicit.

The third force was change itself. Change had become pervasive, persistent and faster. Product life cycles were shrinking. In some industries, products that once had lifetimes of several years were being replaced in months. Technology changed the nature of products, processes and markets. Hammer and Champy argued that organizations built for stability and control could not cope with continuous change because their structures, measures and cultures all resisted it. A functional hierarchy was efficient at doing the same thing many times, but it was very slow to learn and adapt.

For manufacturing and logistics, the three Cs translated into a specific set of pressures that are still familiar. Customers demanded shorter lead times and smaller orders. Competition pushed prices down and quality up. Change shortened product life cycles and made forecasting harder. A factory designed for long runs of standard products, fed by large inventories and controlled by central planning, struggled to respond. The authors used this context to argue that companies needed to stop asking how to do their existing work better and start asking why they did that work at all. Once the environment had changed so fundamentally, incremental improvement of an obsolete model would never close the gap.

## The Definition: Fundamental, Radical, Dramatic, Process

At the heart of the book is a definition that became one of the most quoted sentences in management literature. Reengineering, the authors wrote, is the "fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes" in order to achieve dramatic improvements in critical measures of performance such as cost, quality, service and speed. The authors then explained that four key words carried the meaning of this definition: fundamental, radical, dramatic and process. Each of them deserves attention, because together they distinguished reengineering from almost every other improvement method then in use.

The word fundamental meant asking the most basic questions about the business. Instead of asking how to do a task more efficiently, the reengineer was to ask why the task was done at all, and why it was done in that particular way. The authors stressed that this questioning should start with no assumptions and no givens. Many companies, they noted, discovered that a large part of their work existed only because of rules and assumptions that had once made sense but had become obsolete. An inspection step might exist because of a quality problem that had been solved years ago. A multi level approval might exist because managers once lacked information that was now available to everyone. Asking fundamental questions brought these hidden rules to the surface.

The word radical came from the Latin word for root. Radical redesign meant getting to the root of things, discarding existing structures and procedures and inventing completely new ways of accomplishing the work. It was explicitly opposed to superficial change, to improving or enhancing or modifying what already existed. In one of the book's most memorable formulations, reengineering was about reinvention rather than improvement. For an operations manager this was a challenging message, because most of the toolkit of industrial engineering, from time and motion studies to line balancing, was designed to improve an existing configuration.

The word dramatic referred to the scale of the improvements sought. Reengineering was not for companies that needed to get ten percent better. It was for companies that needed order of magnitude improvements, what the authors described as quantum leaps in performance. Marginal improvement required fine tuning; dramatic improvement required blowing up the old and replacing it with something new. The authors suggested that three kinds of companies undertook reengineering: those in deep trouble, those not yet in trouble but whose management could see trouble coming, and those at the peak of their performance who wanted to stay ahead of competitors by raising the bar.

The fourth and most important word was process. The authors argued that this was the one that gave managers the most difficulty, because most of them were not process oriented. They were focused on tasks, jobs, people and structures. Hammer and Champy defined a business process as a collection of activities that takes one or more kinds of input and creates an output that is of value to the customer. Delivering an order to a customer was a process. The individual tasks within it, such as entering the order, picking the goods, packing the shipment and preparing the invoice, were not processes in themselves. They had no value to the customer unless the whole process worked. The trouble with the functional organization was that it was built around tasks and departments, so nobody saw, measured or managed the process as a whole.

This shift from tasks to processes was the conceptual core of the book. It is also where the influence of industrial thinking is most visible. A process in the Hammer and Champy sense is analogous to a material flow in a factory: inputs enter, are transformed through a sequence of steps and leave as outputs for a customer. Operations specialists had long analyzed flows of material; the book's contribution was to insist that the same view should apply to orders, information, decisions and documents, and that the entire company should be designed around these flows rather than around functional departments. Later writers would call this the horizontal view of the organization, in contrast to the vertical view of the organization chart.

The authors also insisted that reengineering was not the same as several things it was often confused with. It was not automation, which merely made existing processes faster. It was not software reengineering, which rebuilt old information systems with newer technology. It was not restructuring or downsizing, which cut capacity to meet lower demand while doing the same work with fewer people. It was not reorganizing or flattening the hierarchy, because the problem lay in the processes, not the structure. And it was not quality improvement or total quality management, which they regarded as a valuable but incremental approach working within existing processes. These distinctions would later become important in the debate about whether reengineering was responsible for mass layoffs.

## Processes in Manufacturing and Logistics

One of the most practical contributions of the book was its advice on how to identify a company's processes. Hammer and Champy observed that few companies had ever named their processes, and that most managers, when asked to list them, produced a list of departments or functions. They suggested that processes should be named in a way that describes their start and end states, because the name itself forces people to think across departmental boundaries. Thus manufacturing could be seen as running from procurement to shipment, product development from concept to prototype, sales from prospect to order, order fulfillment from order to payment and service from inquiry to resolution. In their experience, even very large companies could describe their work in terms of a relatively small number of major processes, often somewhere between a handful and about twenty.

Order fulfillment is perhaps the clearest example of what the authors meant, and it sits at the center of manufacturing and logistics. In a traditional company, order fulfillment touches almost every department. A salesperson takes the order, customer service enters it, the credit department checks the customer, production planning schedules it, purchasing buys materials, the plant makes the product, quality control inspects it, the warehouse stores and picks it, the traffic department arranges transport, shipping dispatches it and accounting invoices and collects payment. Each department has its own systems, its own objectives and its own sense of priority. The order may spend most of its life waiting between steps. The authors repeatedly made the point that in such processes the actual work time is a tiny fraction of the elapsed time, a finding that matched what industrial engineers and lean practitioners measured on factory floors, where value adding time is often a very small percentage of total lead time.

Seen as a process, order fulfillment becomes a single flow with a single customer and a single set of measures: how fast, how accurately and at what cost an order is turned into a delivered product and a received payment. Once the process is seen in this way, many of the handoffs, checks and delays appear unnecessary. Credit checks can be done automatically against limits in a shared database. Orders can be entered once and visible to everyone. Production can be scheduled on the basis of real orders rather than forecasts. Shipping and invoicing can be triggered by the same event. In the reengineered vision, a small team or even a single case manager can take responsibility for the order from beginning to end, supported by information systems that give them access to everything they need.

Procurement is another process that the book treated at length, most famously through the Ford accounts payable example discussed in the next chapter. In the traditional model, purchasing, receiving and accounts payable each handled their own document: the purchase order, the receiving report and the supplier invoice. Accounts payable had to match the three documents, and much of its work consisted of investigating discrepancies. The reengineering insight was that the entire matching activity was a consequence of the way information was divided among departments. If the purchase order lived in a shared database and receiving simply confirmed the arrival of the goods against it, payment could follow without an invoice and without matching.

Product development was the third major operations process in the book. In many manufacturing companies, development was organized sequentially. Marketing defined the product, design engineering created the design, manufacturing engineering figured out how to produce it, purchasing found suppliers and the plant built it. Each group waited for the previous group to finish, and problems discovered late in the sequence sent the design back to the beginning. The authors described this as throwing the design over the wall. Reengineered product development, as in the Kodak case, replaced the sequence with parallel work by integrated teams sharing a common design database, an approach generally known as concurrent or simultaneous engineering.

The manufacturing process itself, from procurement to shipment, received less detailed treatment in the book than one might expect, partly because the authors believed that factory floors had already been improved significantly through automation and quality programs, while the administrative processes that surrounded them had been neglected. Hammer often argued that the greatest opportunities lay in the white collar work that wrapped around production: order handling, planning, scheduling, purchasing, logistics coordination and invoicing. In many companies the physical production time for a product was measured in hours or days while the administrative process to get an order into production and out to the customer took weeks. This observation was one of the reasons reengineering became so closely tied to supply chain management and to enterprise systems in the years that followed.

Logistics, finally, appears in the book mainly through retail and distribution examples, especially Wal-Mart's relationship with Procter and Gamble. Here the process boundary extended beyond the company itself. The authors pointed out that the most powerful redesigns often involved rethinking the division of work between a company and its suppliers or customers. When a supplier takes responsibility for managing the inventory in a customer's warehouse or store, using shared sales data, entire activities such as order placement, order confirmation and stock checking disappear from both sides. This idea of interorganizational process redesign anticipated much of the later work on supply chain integration, collaborative planning and vendor managed inventory.

## The Case Studies: IBM Credit, Ford, Kodak and Others

Much of the book's persuasive power came from its stories. Hammer and Champy illustrated each principle with examples drawn from real companies, told in a vivid narrative style. Several of these cases became so famous that they were repeated in business school classrooms and consulting presentations for decades. Although not all of them are manufacturing stories in the narrow sense, each of them carries a lesson about how work flows, and several are directly about industrial operations.

The first and most famous case is IBM Credit Corporation, the subsidiary that financed the computers, software and services IBM sold. In its original process, a request for financing passed through a sequence of specialist departments. One logged the request, another checked the customer's creditworthiness, another adapted the standard loan agreement to the customer's terms, another determined the interest rate and a final group assembled the quote. The whole process took on average about six days and sometimes as long as two weeks, during which the sale was at risk because the customer could look elsewhere. Two senior managers decided to walk a request through every step themselves, asking each department to handle it immediately. They found that the actual work took only about ninety minutes. The rest was waiting and handing off. The specialist structure assumed that every request was complex and required expert judgment at each step, but most requests were routine. IBM Credit replaced the specialists with generalists, called deal structurers, who handled each request from beginning to end with the help of a computer system that gave them access to the necessary data and tools. Specialists remained available for the difficult cases. The result, as the authors told it, was a reduction in turnaround of around ninety percent and an enormous increase in the number of deals the unit could handle with a smaller staff. For operations thinkers, the lesson is identical to one taught on factory floors: the bottleneck is not in the processing but in the queues between processing steps.

The second famous case is Ford's accounts payable department, and it is the clearest procurement example in the book. In the early 1980s, Ford's North American accounts payable operation employed more than five hundred people. Management hoped to reduce this by about twenty percent through rationalization and new systems. Then Ford studied Mazda, in which it held a significant stake, and discovered that Mazda's accounts payable function was run by a handful of people. Even allowing for Mazda's smaller size, the gap was enormous. Ford realized that improving the existing process would never close it. In the old process, purchasing sent a purchase order to the supplier and a copy to accounts payable. When the goods arrived at the dock, receiving sent a receiving document to accounts payable. The supplier sent an invoice. Accounts payable clerks then had to match fourteen data items across the three documents, and most of their time was spent investigating mismatches. The reengineered process introduced what came to be called invoiceless processing. Purchasing entered the order into an online database. When goods arrived, the receiving clerk checked the database. If the delivery matched an outstanding order, the clerk accepted it and the system automatically authorized payment. If it did not match, the goods were sent back. The invoice disappeared, and with it the matching work. According to the book, Ford achieved a reduction of around seventy five percent in headcount in the relevant operation, along with simpler material control and more accurate financial information. The lesson the authors drew was that Ford had not reengineered the accounts payable department; it had reengineered the procurement process, and the department's work shrank as a consequence.

The Kodak case concerns product development in manufacturing. In the late 1980s, Fuji introduced a single use 35 millimeter camera, and Kodak had no competing product ready. Kodak's traditional development process was sequential, with each part of the camera designed in turn and then handed to manufacturing engineering. Estimates put the time to market at about seventy weeks. Kodak redesigned the process around an integrated computer aided design and manufacturing database. Engineers working on different parts of the camera worked simultaneously on the same shared design, which was updated each day. Conflicts between components were identified immediately rather than discovered at the end. Manufacturing engineers began designing tooling while product design was still in progress. According to the authors, development time was cut nearly in half, to under forty weeks, and tooling and manufacturing costs fell substantially. This is a classic example of concurrent engineering enabled by shared information, and it shows how reengineering principles applied to industrial product development.

The book also described Hewlett-Packard's purchasing process. HP had dozens of manufacturing units, each with its own purchasing department. This decentralization gave each unit responsiveness and control but meant that HP failed to exploit its total buying power across suppliers. Traditional thinking offered a choice between centralization, with its economies of scale, and decentralization, with its flexibility. HP refused the trade off. It created a shared database of approved suppliers and negotiated contract terms, managed by a corporate procurement group, while buyers in each unit continued to place their own orders using that database. The result, according to the book, combined the bargaining power of a centralized organization with the speed of decentralized units, leading to lower material costs, shorter lead times and better delivery performance. The case became a model for later shared service and category management designs in procurement.

Wal-Mart's relationship with Procter and Gamble appears in the book as an example of redesign that crosses company boundaries. Instead of Wal-Mart's buyers monitoring stock and placing replenishment orders, Procter and Gamble received point of sale and inventory data from Wal-Mart and took responsibility for replenishing Wal-Mart's distribution centers. It decided when and how much to ship, and it was paid when the goods were sold. This arrangement, widely known as vendor managed inventory, removed duplicate planning and ordering activity, reduced inventories and improved availability. Combined with Wal-Mart's cross docking practice, in which goods arriving at distribution centers are transferred directly to outbound trucks without being stored, it became one of the most cited examples of logistics integration in the 1990s.

Other cases filled out the picture. Mutual Benefit Life, an insurer, replaced a long chain of clerks and departments in its policy issuing process with case managers who handled an application from beginning to end, cutting turnaround from weeks to days. Bell Atlantic redesigned the process of connecting long distance carriers to its local network, which had taken weeks, into one run by case teams that could do it in a fraction of the time. Taco Bell rethought its restaurants around the idea of a kitchen with minimal food preparation on site, moving preparation to central facilities and freeing space and labor for customers, a change the company associated with large growth in sales. Hallmark rethought its greeting card development process to bring products to market much faster. Capital Holding, a financial services company, reorganized around customer segments and processes rather than products. Although these examples come from service industries, they rest on the same operational logic of flow, handoffs and integrated responsibility that applies on a factory floor.

It is worth noting that the numbers in these cases come from the authors' accounts and from the companies themselves, as told at the time. Later researchers pointed out that some of the cases were simplified and that several of the companies involved went through difficult periods afterwards. Nonetheless, the cases achieved their purpose. They showed managers concrete, memorable examples of what it meant to redesign a process rather than improve a task, and they gave a generation of consultants a shared repertoire of stories.

## Recurring Themes of Reengineered Processes

After presenting its cases, the book drew out a set of characteristics that tended to appear in reengineered processes. The authors were careful to say that these were not rules to apply mechanically but patterns they had observed again and again. Taken together, they form something like a design vocabulary for processes, and nearly every one of them has a direct counterpart in manufacturing and logistics thinking.

The first theme was that several jobs are combined into one. Instead of an assembly line of specialists, each performing one small task and passing the work on, a single person or a small team performs the whole sequence. The IBM Credit deal structurer and the Mutual Benefit Life case manager are examples. The benefit comes from eliminating handoffs, with their delays, errors and coordination costs, and from giving one person clear ownership of the result. In manufacturing, the same idea appears in cellular manufacturing, where a small team of multiskilled operators builds a complete product or subassembly in a compact cell rather than performing a single operation on a long line. It is a direct reversal of the Smith and Ford logic of the division of labor, at least for work where coordination costs outweigh the gains of specialization.

The second theme was that workers make decisions. In the traditional organization, those who did the work and those who decided about it were different people, and decisions traveled up and down the hierarchy. In reengineered processes, decision making became part of the work itself. The authors called this vertical compression, to complement the horizontal compression of combining jobs. When workers have the information and the authority to decide, delays shrink, overhead falls and responsiveness improves. On the factory floor, this parallels the Toyota practice of allowing any worker to stop the line when a problem appears and the broader movement toward self directed work teams.

The third theme was that steps are performed in their natural order. Traditional processes imposed a strict linear sequence, often for no reason other than that one department's work came before another's. Reengineered processes asked which steps truly depended on each other and allowed the others to proceed in parallel. The authors called this delinearizing the process. Kodak's simultaneous design of camera parts and tooling is the clearest case. In operations language, this is about the difference between real precedence constraints and artificial ones, and about compressing lead time through overlapping activities.

The fourth theme was that processes have multiple versions. The mass production mindset favored a single standard process for all cases, which inevitably became complex because it had to handle every exception. Reengineered processes instead used several versions, each tailored to a different type of case, with a triage step at the front to route work to the right version. Simple orders follow a fast, simple path; complex orders follow a path with more expertise. This idea is closely related to the operations concept of segmenting product flows by volume and variability, sometimes described as separating runners, repeaters and strangers, and to the design of focused factories.

The fifth theme was that work is performed where it makes the most sense. Functional organizations tended to send every task of a particular kind to the department that specialized in it, so that, for example, all purchasing went through the purchasing department even for small, routine items. Reengineered processes moved work across organizational boundaries to wherever it could be done most effectively. A department might buy its own small items, or a customer or supplier might take on a task. Vendor managed inventory is a striking example, in which the supplier performs the replenishment decision that the customer used to make. The broader principle, that organizational boundaries should not dictate where work is done, became one of the foundations of supply chain thinking.

The sixth theme was that checks and controls are reduced. Traditional processes were filled with verification steps designed to prevent errors and abuse. The authors argued that controls should be used only where they make economic sense. Often, the cost of checking every transaction exceeds the cost of the errors it prevents. Reengineered processes used aggregate or deferred controls, tolerating modest abuse in exchange for large savings. This idea resembles the quality movement's shift from inspecting every part at the end of the line to building quality into the process, and also its insistence that inspection adds no value in itself.

The seventh theme was that reconciliation is minimized. Reconciliation, the work of matching information from different sources, was in the authors' view a symptom of fragmentation. If two departments record the same transaction separately, someone must reconcile the two records. Reengineered processes reduced the number of external contact points and the number of places where data was captured, so that there was less to match. Ford's elimination of three way matching is the textbook case. In modern terms, this is the argument for a single source of truth, which became central to enterprise resource planning.

The eighth theme was that a case manager provides a single point of contact. When a process was too complex to be integrated into a single person's job, the authors recommended appointing a case manager who acted as a buffer between the complex process and the customer. The case manager had access to all the information systems and people involved and could answer the customer's questions and resolve problems as if responsible for the whole process. In industrial companies this role often appears as a key account manager or an order manager who follows a customer order through engineering, production and delivery.

The ninth and final theme was that hybrid centralized and decentralized operations become prevalent. Information technology allowed companies to combine the advantages of central coordination and local autonomy rather than choosing between them. Hewlett-Packard's purchasing model is the prime example. Shared databases, telecommunications networks and standardized systems made it possible for local units to act independently while the company as a whole still benefited from scale and visibility. This principle became the basis for many later designs of global manufacturing networks, shared services centers and centrally coordinated but locally executed logistics.

Taken together, these themes describe a profound redesign of operational work. They point toward fewer handoffs, shorter lead times, integrated responsibility, flexible routing of work and information shared rather than duplicated. Many of them overlap with what Japanese manufacturers had been doing on factory floors for years. What was new in Hammer and Champy was the generalization of these ideas from the physical flow of goods to the whole enterprise, including its administrative, financial and commercial processes.

## Information Technology and the Breaking of Rules

Information technology plays a central role in the book, but in a way that was more subtle than many readers noticed. Hammer and Champy called it an essential enabler of reengineering, and they warned at the same time that it was routinely misused. Most companies, they argued, used technology to mechanize old ways of doing business. They put computers into existing processes, making the same steps happen faster, but leaving the fragmentation intact. This was the meaning of Hammer's earlier slogan about obliterating rather than automating. Paving a cow path, as the familiar expression put it, only gives you a faster cow path.

The authors explained this misuse in terms of how managers think about technology. Most managers think deductively. They define a problem and then look for a solution to it, including technological solutions. But the real power of new technology, the authors argued, lies in its ability to make possible things that nobody had thought of as problems, because nobody had imagined they could be done differently. Reengineering therefore required inductive thinking: the ability to recognize a powerful technology and then seek out the problems it might solve, often problems the company did not yet know it had. They compared this to the history of innovations such as the photocopier, whose market was underestimated because people judged it against their existing needs for copies.

The most memorable part of this discussion is the idea of disruptive technologies that break old rules. The authors identified a series of deeply held assumptions about how work had to be done, each of them rooted in the limitations of earlier technology, and showed how a particular technology made the assumption obsolete. The first rule was that information can appear in only one place at a time. Shared databases broke it, allowing information to appear simultaneously wherever it was needed. This is the logic behind Ford's invoiceless procurement and Kodak's shared design database, and it is the essence of what enterprise resource planning systems would later offer across manufacturing, logistics and finance.

The second rule was that only experts can perform complex work. Expert systems, which were fashionable at the time, suggested that a generalist supported by software could do work that once required a specialist. This was the logic behind the IBM Credit deal structurer. The third rule was that businesses must choose between centralization and decentralization. Telecommunications networks allowed companies to enjoy the benefits of both, as Hewlett-Packard's purchasing showed. The fourth rule was that managers make all the decisions. Decision support tools, including database access and modeling software, allowed decision making to become part of everyone's job.

The fifth rule was that field personnel need offices where they can receive, store, retrieve and transmit information. Wireless data communication and portable computers made it possible for field staff, such as service engineers and sales representatives, to work from wherever they were. The sixth rule was that the best contact with a potential buyer is personal contact. Interactive media, which at the time meant technologies such as interactive videodisks, suggested that the best contact was simply effective contact, whatever form it took.

The seventh and eighth rules are the most directly relevant to logistics and manufacturing. The seventh was that you have to find out where things are. Automatic identification and tracking technology, such as bar codes and early radio frequency identification and satellite tracking of vehicles, meant that things could tell you where they were. A trucking company could know the location of every truck, and a warehouse could know the location of every pallet, without anyone searching or telephoning. This idea underpinned the transformation of distribution centers, transport management and inventory control in the 1990s and 2000s. The eighth rule was that plans get revised periodically. High performance computing made it possible to revise plans instantaneously as conditions changed. For a manufacturer, this pointed toward production plans and schedules that could react to real orders and real capacity in near real time, rather than being recalculated weekly or monthly in batch runs of material requirements planning.

Read today, the list of technologies is dated, but the reasoning behind it has aged well. Every subsequent wave of technology has been discussed in the same terms. Enterprise systems, the internet, electronic data interchange and later web based supplier portals, mobile computing, cloud platforms, the internet of things, advanced planning systems and artificial intelligence have all been described as tools that either automate the existing process or make a new process possible. The persistent finding of later research, that technology investments deliver their largest returns when they are combined with changes in processes and organization, is essentially the thesis of this chapter of the book.

## People, Jobs, Organization and Values

Although reengineering is often remembered as a technical exercise in redrawing flow charts, Hammer and Champy argued that a redesigned process changes almost everything about how a company works. To explain this, they used a model they called the business system diamond. At one corner are business processes. The way work is done determines the nature of jobs and organizational structures, which form the second corner. The kinds of jobs and structures determine the kinds of management and measurement systems needed, the third corner. Those systems in turn shape the values and beliefs of the people in the organization, the fourth corner, and those values support or undermine the processes, closing the loop. The point of the diamond was that changing processes without changing the other three corners would fail, because the old jobs, measures and culture would pull the organization back to its old ways.

The authors then described a series of shifts that occur when a company reengineers. Work units change from functional departments to process teams. A process team is a group of people who together perform a complete process, or a substantial part of it. The authors distinguished between case teams, which bring together the skills needed to handle a type of case repeatedly, and virtual teams, which come together temporarily to handle a particular case and then dissolve. In manufacturing, the equivalent might be a product focused cell or a value stream team that includes production operators, planners, quality specialists and maintenance staff, rather than separate departments for each.

Jobs change from simple tasks to multidimensional work. Process team members share responsibility for results, and they must understand the whole process, not only their own step. The work becomes more varied, more challenging and more demanding. The authors were frank that not everyone would welcome this. Some people prefer simple, well defined tasks, and reengineered work requires more skill and more judgment. People's roles change from controlled to empowered. Workers in a reengineered process are expected to make decisions and follow their own judgment within the boundaries of the process, rather than follow rules set by supervisors.

Job preparation shifts from training to education. Training teaches people how to perform a specific task; education increases their insight and understanding so that they can figure out what to do in situations nobody anticipated. The focus of performance measures and compensation shifts from activity to results. Instead of being paid for time spent or tasks completed, people are measured and rewarded for the value their process creates for customers, and the authors discussed bonuses based on team and process performance. Advancement criteria shift from performance to ability. In the old organization, promotion was a reward for good work in the current job. In the new one, the authors argued, promotion should be based on the ability to do the new job, and good performance in the current job should be rewarded with pay and recognition rather than with a move into management.

Values change from protective to productive. In a fragmented organization, employees learn to protect themselves, to follow procedures, to please the boss and to avoid blame. In a process organization, they need to believe that the customer pays their salaries, that every job is essential and important, that showing up is not an accomplishment and that the team succeeds or fails together. Managers change from supervisors to coaches. When teams manage their own work, the manager's role becomes one of developing people, providing resources and removing obstacles. Organizational structures change from hierarchical to flat, because there is less need for layers whose job was to coordinate fragmented work and pass information up and down. Finally, executives change from scorekeepers to leaders. Instead of monitoring financial results from a distance, executives must shape the values and beliefs of the organization through their words and actions.

The authors also introduced the role of the process owner as a permanent feature of the reengineered company. A process owner is a senior manager responsible for the performance and design of a process across all the departments it touches. In a manufacturing company, an order fulfillment process owner would be accountable for lead time, delivery reliability and cost across sales, planning, production, logistics and billing. Hammer would develop this idea much further in his later work, describing companies in which process owners have real authority over process design and measurement, sharing power with traditional functional and business unit managers in a kind of matrix.

This part of the book is, in hindsight, the most humane and also the most neglected. Hammer and Champy described a workplace in which jobs are bigger, more interesting and more responsible, in which people are educated rather than drilled and in which managers coach rather than command. Yet they also accepted, quite openly, that reengineering would eliminate many jobs, particularly those of middle managers and clerks whose work consisted of coordinating fragments. The tension between these two sides of the message would later dominate the public reaction to the book.

## How Reengineering Is Done: Roles, Process Maps and Selection

Hammer and Champy did not present a rigid methodology, and they were skeptical of consultants who reduced reengineering to a sequence of standard steps. They did, however, describe how successful efforts were organized, who was involved and how companies chose where to start. This practical side of the book shaped the way many industrial companies ran their programs in the 1990s.

The authors identified several roles. The leader was a senior executive, often the chief executive, who authorized and motivated the overall effort. Because reengineering cut across departments and threatened established power, it could not be led from the middle of the organization. The leader's job was to create the vision, to make the case for change, to appoint process owners and to keep the effort going when resistance appeared. The process owner, as described earlier, was a manager with responsibility for a specific process and for its reengineering. The reengineering team was a small group, typically somewhere around five to ten people, dedicated to redesigning a particular process. The authors recommended mixing insiders, who knew the current process and had credibility with colleagues, with outsiders, who did not know the process and were therefore free to ask naive questions. A steering committee of senior managers could help set priorities and resolve conflicts between projects. Finally, a person the authors called the reengineering czar was responsible for developing techniques and tools and for coordinating the different projects across the company.

The first concrete task was to build a process map. This was not a detailed flow chart of every activity but a high level picture of the company's major processes and how they related to each other. The authors described the case of a semiconductor business that, after much discussion, described itself in terms of a small number of processes such as strategy development, product development, customer design support, manufacturing capability development, customer communication and order fulfillment. The very act of drawing such a map, they said, often changed how managers understood their business, because it showed work flowing horizontally across the boxes of the organization chart.

The second task was to select which processes to reengineer first. The book proposed three criteria. The first was dysfunction: which processes were in the deepest trouble? Signs of dysfunction included extensive information exchange, data redundancy and rekeying, which indicated arbitrary fragmentation; large inventories, buffers and other assets, which indicated slack used to compensate for uncertainty; a high ratio of checking and control to value adding work, which indicated fragmentation and distrust; rework and iteration, which indicated inadequate feedback along the chain; and complexity, exceptions and special cases, which indicated accretion onto a simple base. For manufacturing managers, the second symptom is especially telling. The authors were essentially saying that excess inventory is a symptom of a poorly designed process, a view that matches the lean principle that inventory hides problems.

The second criterion was importance: which processes had the greatest impact on the company's customers? The third was feasibility: which processes were most likely to be redesigned successfully, given their scope, the expected cost, the strength of the team and the commitment of the owner? A good first project, the authors suggested, would score well on all three.

The third task was to understand the selected process. Here the book departed sharply from traditional systems analysis. The authors warned against analyzing the existing process in exhaustive detail, because such analysis consumed time and tended to trap the team in the logic of the old design. Instead, the team needed to understand what the process did, how well it performed and what critical issues governed its performance. The starting point should be the customer: what the customer really needed, what the customer did with the output and what problems the customer faced. They encouraged teams to observe customers directly and even to spend time working in the customer's environment.

The fourth task was the redesign itself. The book described this as the most creative and least structured part of the effort, requiring imagination, inductive thinking and a willingness to break rules. Teams could use the recurring themes as a source of ideas and could ask how each of the disruptive technologies might change the process. They could also look for the assumptions buried in the current process and ask what would happen if each were removed. The authors suggested that redesign sessions should be energetic and playful, with no idea dismissed too early. Once a promising design had emerged, it needed to be tested through pilots and refined before being rolled out across the organization.

In practice, many companies combined this approach with techniques from industrial engineering and quality management, such as flow charting, cycle time analysis, activity based costing and benchmarking. Consulting firms developed their own branded methods. Over time, the original emphasis on creativity and radical ideas tended to give way to more structured, analytical project management, which some critics felt diluted the original vision but which also made the approach easier to apply at scale.

## Why Reengineering Fails

One of the most striking features of the book is how openly it discussed failure. Hammer and Champy estimated that somewhere between half and seventy percent of the organizations that undertook reengineering did not achieve the dramatic results they intended. This figure would later be quoted over and over, often out of context, as proof that reengineering did not work. The authors' own interpretation was different. They argued that failure was not inherent in the idea but resulted from avoidable mistakes in how companies went about it. They devoted a full chapter to these mistakes, and the list remains a useful checklist for any large transformation program, in manufacturing or elsewhere.

The most common mistake was to try to fix a process instead of changing it. Teams that started by analyzing the existing process in detail often ended up making incremental improvements, removing a step here and automating a step there, while leaving the fundamental design untouched. A related mistake was not focusing on business processes at all: companies launched programs called reengineering that were in fact reorganizations, cost cutting exercises or quality programs, organized around departments rather than processes.

A third mistake was to ignore everything except process redesign. As the business system diamond showed, new processes required new jobs, structures, measurement systems and values. Companies that changed the flow chart but kept the old job descriptions, incentives and management styles saw the new processes fail in practice. Closely linked was the mistake of neglecting people's values and beliefs. People needed reasons to work in new ways, and those reasons had to be reinforced by what management said, did and rewarded.

Several mistakes concerned ambition. Some companies were willing to settle for minor results, declaring victory after modest improvements and missing the larger opportunity. Others quit too early, abandoning the effort when the first difficulties appeared or when early results were disappointing. Some placed prior constraints on the definition of the problem and the scope of the effort, ruling out changes to particular departments, systems or relationships before the redesign had even started. Others allowed existing corporate cultures and management attitudes to prevent reengineering from getting started, for example a culture of consensus that made radical change impossible or a short term orientation that could not wait for results.

Another group of mistakes concerned leadership and organization. The authors were emphatic that reengineering could not be made to happen from the bottom up. People in the middle and lower levels of an organization lacked the broad perspective and the authority to redesign processes that cut across departments, and middle managers in particular were likely to resist changes that threatened their positions. Assigning someone who did not understand reengineering to lead the effort was another fatal mistake, as was skimping on the resources devoted to it. Burying reengineering in the middle of the corporate agenda, so that it competed with dozens of other initiatives, meant it would receive neither attention nor commitment. Dissipating energy across too many projects at once had a similar effect.

The authors also mentioned some more specific traps. Attempting to reengineer when the chief executive was close to retirement was dangerous, because the effort needed a leader who would be around long enough to see it through. Failing to distinguish reengineering from other business improvement programs led to confusion and cynicism among employees who had already lived through several initiatives. Concentrating exclusively on design, without attention to implementation, produced elegant plans that were never realized. Trying to make reengineering happen without making anybody unhappy was unrealistic, because the effort inevitably threatened established interests. Pulling back when people resisted was fatal, since resistance was natural and predictable. Finally, dragging the effort out for too long exhausted the organization; the authors suggested that a reengineering project should move from launch to first results within a year or so.

From an operations perspective, the failure list reads like an early version of the lessons that would later be learned from large ERP implementations, lean transformations and digital programs. The recurring themes are the need for senior sponsorship, the importance of changing measures and incentives along with processes, the danger of scope creep and dissipated energy, the trap of automating existing processes, and the underestimation of human resistance. The irony, which critics were quick to point out, is that the authors' frank admission of high failure rates was turned against them, while their explanation of why failures occurred was frequently ignored.

## Reengineering, Quality Management and the Japanese Model

Reengineering did not appear in an intellectual vacuum. It arrived at the end of a decade in which Western manufacturers had been studying Japanese production methods with an intensity that bordered on obsession. To understand the book's place in the history of operations management, it has to be set alongside total quality management, the Toyota Production System and the lean manufacturing movement, as well as other process oriented ideas of the time.

Total quality management had its roots in the work of W. Edwards Deming, Joseph Juran and others, and it had been adopted enthusiastically by Japanese industry after the Second World War before returning to the West in the 1980s. It emphasized customer focus, statistical process control, the reduction of variation, employee involvement and continuous improvement. Hammer and Champy treated quality programs with respect, but they drew a sharp line between them and reengineering. Quality programs, in their view, worked within the framework of existing processes and sought to improve them through incremental, continuous change. Reengineering sought breakthroughs by discarding existing processes and replacing them with entirely new ones. The authors suggested that the two were complementary: a company might reengineer a process to achieve a step change and then apply quality methods to improve the new process continuously until the time came for another redesign. Many practitioners and academics described this in terms of a curve of steady incremental improvement punctuated by occasional radical jumps.

The Toyota Production System, developed by Taiichi Ohno and colleagues from the late 1940s onward, was the most important source of operational innovation in the period. It was built on just in time production, in which each process produces only what the next process needs, when it needs it, and on jidoka, the principle of building quality into the process by stopping production when an abnormality occurs. Its tools included kanban pull systems, quick changeovers, level scheduling, standardized work and the relentless elimination of waste in its many forms: overproduction, waiting, transport, overprocessing, inventory, motion and defects. In 1990, the book The Machine That Changed the World by James Womack, Daniel Jones and Daniel Roos, based on the MIT International Motor Vehicle Program, popularized the term lean production for this system and showed the large performance gap between the best Japanese plants and their Western competitors.

The overlap between reengineering and lean thinking is considerable. Both focused on flow rather than on individual tasks. Both regarded waiting time, handoffs, inspection and inventory as symptoms of poor design. Both encouraged multiskilled workers, small integrated teams and decision making at the point of work. Both looked at the work from the customer's point of view. When Womack and Jones published Lean Thinking in 1996, their concept of the value stream, the set of all actions required to bring a product from concept to launch, from order to delivery and from raw material to the customer, was very close to Hammer and Champy's concept of a process. They even described the occasional need for kaikaku, radical improvement, alongside the continuous improvement of kaizen.

There were also important differences. Lean thinking grew out of the factory floor, from the bottom up, and emphasized the involvement of frontline workers in a long, patient process of learning and improvement. It placed great value on stability, standardization and visual management, and it was generally skeptical of complex information systems, preferring simple physical signals such as kanban cards. Reengineering, by contrast, came from the executive suite and from information technology consulting. It was top down by design, as the book made clear, and it relied heavily on shared databases and computer systems. Lean practitioners tended to see reengineering as a blunt instrument that ignored the knowledge of workers and destroyed trust by cutting jobs. Reengineering advocates tended to see lean and quality programs as too slow and too narrow, confined to the factory while the real waste lay in the administrative processes around it.

Other thinkers of the period were working on related ideas. Thomas Davenport and James Short published an article in 1990 on what they called the new industrial engineering, arguing for the redesign of business processes using information technology, and Davenport's 1993 book Process Innovation offered a more measured and analytical treatment of similar themes. Eliyahu Goldratt's theory of constraints, popularized through his novel The Goal in 1984, focused attention on bottlenecks and throughput across the whole production system. George Stalk and Thomas Hout's work on time based competition, published in 1990, argued that speed across the entire chain of activities was a decisive source of advantage, an argument that reengineering echoed. In this sense, Reengineering the Corporation was part of a broad shift in management thinking from optimizing functions to optimizing flows.

In later years, the frontier between these schools became blurred. Many companies combined lean tools with process redesign and information systems. Six Sigma, developed at Motorola and made famous by General Electric in the second half of the 1990s, added statistical rigor and a structured project method. By the 2000s, the combination known as Lean Six Sigma had become the dominant improvement approach in manufacturing, and its practitioners routinely spoke of process owners, end to end processes and redesign, terms that had been popularized by Hammer and Champy.

## Impact on Industry: Consultancies, Enterprise Systems, Supply Chains and Downsizing

The impact of the book on industry was immediate and enormous. Within two or three years of its publication, surveys of large companies in the United States and Europe reported that a majority were engaged in some form of reengineering, or at least described their change programs with that word. Annual reports, analyst presentations and press releases announced reengineering initiatives. Business magazines put the term on their covers. The word entered ordinary language, sometimes as a symbol of modern management and sometimes as a synonym for job cuts.

The first beneficiary was the consulting industry. CSC Index, Champy's firm, grew rapidly and became, for a few years, one of the most fashionable consultancies in the world. The large accounting based consulting practices of the time, such as Andersen Consulting, Ernst and Young, Price Waterhouse, Coopers and Lybrand, Deloitte and KPMG, built large reengineering practices, as did strategy firms and information technology service companies. Reengineering offered them something extremely attractive: a mandate to examine the entire enterprise, a justification for large multiyear engagements, and a natural link to the implementation of new information systems. Spending on reengineering consulting and related technology grew into billions of dollars a year in the mid 1990s. Hammer himself became one of the most sought after speakers in business, running seminars for executives at high fees.

The second and longer lasting impact was the rise of enterprise resource planning. In 1992, the German software company SAP launched R/3, a client server system that integrated finance, controlling, sales and distribution, materials management, production planning, quality management, plant maintenance and human resources in a single database. Competitors such as Oracle, Baan, J.D. Edwards and PeopleSoft offered similar suites. These systems were, in a sense, the technological embodiment of reengineering principles. Their shared database meant that information could appear in many places at once. Their integrated modules linked sales orders directly to production planning, procurement, inventory, shipping and invoicing, which is precisely the end to end order fulfillment process described in the book. Their built in workflows encoded what vendors called best practice processes.

Many manufacturing companies combined reengineering with ERP implementation in large transformation programs. The software forced them to define their processes, standardize data and eliminate local variations, and the reengineering philosophy provided the justification for doing so. At the same time, the relationship was ambiguous. Instead of designing radical new processes and then building systems to support them, many companies ended up adapting their processes to the standard templates of the software. The result was often a kind of reengineering by default, in which the package defined the process. Some implementations delivered large benefits in visibility, inventory reduction and administrative efficiency. Others ran far over budget and over time, and a few well known cases at the end of the 1990s caused serious operational disruptions in order fulfillment and distribution. The urgency created by the year 2000 problem accelerated the adoption of ERP regardless of whether processes had really been rethought.

The third impact was on supply chain management and logistics. The idea that processes cross organizational boundaries, illustrated in the book by Wal-Mart and Procter and Gamble, fed directly into a series of industry initiatives. In the grocery sector, Efficient Consumer Response, launched in 1993, sought to redesign the flow of goods and information from manufacturer to store shelf through continuous replenishment, category management, cross docking and electronic data interchange. In apparel and textiles, Quick Response programs pursued similar goals. Later in the decade, Collaborative Planning, Forecasting and Replenishment extended the concept to shared forecasts between trading partners. Many manufacturers redesigned their distribution networks, consolidated warehouses, outsourced logistics to third party providers and introduced vendor managed inventory with their key customers. The language of processes, cycle times, handoffs and integration that the book had popularized became the standard language of supply chain professionals.

In manufacturing companies, reengineering also reshaped product development and the organization of plants. Automotive manufacturers in the United States and Europe introduced platform teams that brought together design, engineering, purchasing, manufacturing and finance for the development of a vehicle, shortening development times considerably. Suppliers were brought into development earlier and given responsibility for whole systems rather than individual parts. Plants reorganized from functional layouts into product focused cells and value streams, often under the banner of lean manufacturing but with the vocabulary and executive sponsorship that reengineering had made fashionable. Administrative functions such as purchasing, accounts payable, order management and customer service were consolidated, standardized and in many cases moved into shared service centers, an organizational form that drew directly on the book's argument about hybrid centralized and decentralized operations.

The fourth impact, and the most controversial, was on employment. The early and middle 1990s saw a wave of corporate restructuring and layoffs in the United States and Europe, affecting not only factory workers but also, for the first time on a large scale, middle managers and white collar professionals. Many of these cuts were announced as part of reengineering programs, even when they had little to do with process redesign as the book defined it. Reengineering provided both a method and a vocabulary for eliminating layers of management and clerical work, and the book's frank statements that the old jobs of coordination and control would disappear seemed to many readers to confirm that its real purpose was to cut headcount. The word reengineering became, in popular culture, almost synonymous with downsizing, and the anxiety it generated was satirized in newspapers and comic strips.

The ideas also spread beyond private industry. Governments adopted process redesign in tax administration, social security, procurement and public services. In the United States, the National Performance Review launched in 1993 drew on reengineering ideas alongside the broader reinventing government movement. Hospitals, universities and armed forces ran reengineering programs. Wherever large organizations were struggling with slow, fragmented and expensive administrative processes, the book's ideas found an audience.

## Backlash and Reappraisal

By the middle of the 1990s, the mood around reengineering had changed. The term that had sounded so exciting in 1993 had acquired a sinister reputation among employees, and executives were becoming disillusioned with programs that had cost a great deal and delivered less than promised. Academic critics, journalists and even some of the movement's founders began to reassess what had happened.

The most frequent criticism was that reengineering had ignored people. Critics argued that the book treated organizations as machines to be redesigned by engineers, that it underestimated the knowledge embedded in existing employees and practices, and that its rhetoric of radical change, breaking things and starting over legitimized brutal cuts. Thomas Davenport, who had himself been one of the pioneers of process redesign, published an article in 1995 with the pointed title "The Fad That Forgot People," in which he argued that reengineering had become a euphemism for layoffs and had lost sight of the human side of change. Others warned of what Gary Hamel and C. K. Prahalad, in their 1994 book Competing for the Future, described as an excessive focus on cutting the denominator of productivity ratios rather than growing the numerator, a strategy that could leave companies lean but unable to grow. Some observers spoke of corporate anorexia: firms that had cut so deeply that they had lost the slack, experience and informal networks they needed to innovate and to cope with surprises.

The founders themselves acknowledged part of the criticism. In 1995 Champy published Reengineering Management, in which he argued that the biggest obstacle to reengineering had been management itself. Managers had demanded that the rest of the organization change while preserving their own habits, roles and styles. Champy called on them to reengineer their own work, including how they set purpose, built culture, measured performance and led people. In a widely cited interview with The Wall Street Journal in 1996, Hammer conceded that he had not paid enough attention to the human dimension, attributing this to his background as an engineer and saying that he had come to see how critical people were to the success of change. These admissions were reported as a kind of confession and reinforced the impression that reengineering had been a passing fad.

The reputation of the consulting industry also suffered. CSC Index, which had ridden the wave of reengineering, was damaged in 1995 by revelations that it had arranged bulk purchases of a book written by two of its consultants in order to push it onto bestseller lists. The episode was not about Reengineering the Corporation itself, but it contributed to a sense that management ideas were being marketed like consumer products. As the market for reengineering projects declined, the firm lost its prominence and later disappeared as an independent brand.

Research on the actual results of reengineering painted a more mixed picture than either the enthusiasts or the critics suggested. Many projects had failed to achieve their stated goals, especially when they were broad, poorly led or confused with cost cutting. Others had delivered real and lasting improvements in cycle times, quality and cost, especially when they focused on specific processes such as order fulfillment, procurement, claims processing or product development, and when they were combined with investments in information systems and in people. Studies of performance at the process level often found impressive gains, while studies at the level of the whole firm found that gains were frequently diluted by other factors. Economists studying the productivity boom in the United States in the late 1990s concluded that the returns to information technology were highest in companies that had made complementary changes in processes and organization, a finding that gave retrospective support to the book's central thesis even as the word reengineering fell out of fashion.

Hammer himself did not abandon his ideas. In Beyond Reengineering, published in 1996, he described the process centered organization, in which processes rather than functions became the primary axis of management. He argued that the redesign of processes was only the first step and that companies needed to manage processes permanently, with process owners, process measures and a culture of customer focus. In The Agenda (2001) and in later articles, including a well known piece on the process audit in the Harvard Business Review in 2007, he developed tools for assessing process maturity and enterprise capabilities. Until his death in 2008, he continued to argue that the process view of the business was not a fad but a permanent change in how organizations should be managed. Champy, for his part, continued to write about management and later about the application of process thinking to health care and to growth strategies.

The backlash thus had two sides. It exposed the real excesses of the reengineering movement, including the use of the word to justify indiscriminate layoffs, the neglect of human factors and the overselling by consultants. But it also tended to obscure the fact that many of the book's ideas had been absorbed into everyday practice, often under different names. By the end of the decade, few executives wanted to say they were reengineering, but almost all of them were managing processes, implementing integrated systems and redesigning their supply chains.

## Legacy and Conclusion

More than three decades after its publication, Reengineering the Corporation occupies a curious place in the history of management. The word that gave it its title is rarely used now, and when it is, it often carries a faint echo of layoffs and overpromising consultants. Yet the core concepts of the book have become so deeply embedded in industrial practice that they are almost invisible. Few managers today would describe their company only as a collection of departments. Most would recognize that value is created by end to end processes that cross functions and company boundaries, and that the handoffs between departments are where time, money and quality are lost. That way of seeing an organization is the most enduring legacy of Hammer and Champy.

In manufacturing and logistics, this legacy is visible everywhere. The supply chain operations reference model developed by the Supply Chain Council in the late 1990s describes supply chains in terms of processes such as plan, source, make, deliver and return, with standard metrics for each, a framework that closely follows the process logic of the book. Integrated business planning and sales and operations planning bring together sales, marketing, production, procurement and finance in a single monthly process. Order to cash, procure to pay, plan to produce and idea to launch have become standard names for the end to end processes that ERP systems support and that shared service centers operate, and these names follow exactly the convention, proposed in the book, of naming a process by its beginning and end. Vendor managed inventory, cross docking, collaborative forecasting and supplier integration into development are now ordinary practice rather than daring experiments.

The discipline of business process management, which grew in the 2000s, can be seen as the institutionalization of reengineering. It combined process modeling standards, workflow and orchestration software, process performance measurement and governance structures with process owners. More recently, process mining tools have allowed companies to reconstruct their actual processes from the event logs of their information systems, revealing loops, rework, delays and variants in order fulfillment and procurement processes with a precision that the authors could only have imagined. Robotic process automation, which spread in the late 2010s, revived the old debate in a new form. Critics noted that using software robots to copy data between systems was exactly the kind of automation of existing processes that Hammer had warned against, while advocates argued that it provided quick relief while deeper redesign was planned.

The language of digital transformation, which dominated corporate strategy in the 2010s and 2020s, is in many respects a rerun of the reengineering debate. Companies are urged to rethink their business models and processes around new technologies such as cloud computing, connected devices in factories and warehouses, advanced analytics and artificial intelligence, rather than simply digitizing existing procedures. The concept of Industry 4.0, with its vision of connected machines, real time production control and integrated value chains, echoes the book's rules about information that appears everywhere at once, things that tell you where they are and plans that are revised instantaneously. Today's discussions about generative artificial intelligence and autonomous agents return once again to the old question of whether technology will merely speed up existing tasks or allow work to be organized in fundamentally different ways. The warning that automating a fragmented process only produces a faster fragmented process remains as relevant as it was in 1990.

The lessons of the backlash have also become part of the legacy. Modern transformation programs place much greater emphasis on change management, employee involvement, communication and capability building than the reengineering projects of the early 1990s. Lean practitioners have shown that sustained improvement in operations depends on engaging the people who do the work, not only on redesigning their jobs from above. Companies have learned, often painfully, that cutting coordination layers without redesigning the underlying processes simply moves the coordination work elsewhere or leaves it undone. The balance between radical redesign and continuous improvement, between top down vision and bottom up learning, is now generally understood as a matter of combining both rather than choosing one.

In retrospect, the book's greatest strength was also its greatest weakness. Its strength was clarity and audacity. It took ideas that were circulating among operations specialists, quality experts and information systems researchers and presented them to senior executives in a language they could not ignore. It argued convincingly that the fragmentation inherited from Smith, Ford and Sloan had become a liability, that processes rather than tasks should be the unit of design and that information technology could make possible ways of working that had been unthinkable before. Its weakness was the same audacity. The rhetoric of starting over with a clean sheet, of radical and dramatic change, underestimated the value of existing knowledge, relationships and trust, and it was easily captured by those whose real goal was simply to reduce costs.

For anyone working in manufacturing, logistics or operations today, the book remains worth reading, not as a guide to be followed literally, but as a reminder of a few powerful questions. Why do we do this work at all? Who is the customer of this process, and what does that customer really need? How much of the elapsed time is real work, and how much is waiting? Which checks, reconciliations and approvals exist only because information is fragmented? Which rules are based on technological limitations that no longer exist? These questions, asked seriously and answered honestly, are as relevant on a factory floor or in a distribution center in 2026 as they were in the offices of IBM Credit or Ford's accounts payable department more than thirty years ago. In that sense, the revolution that Hammer and Champy proclaimed did take place, though more slowly, more quietly and more humanely than their manifesto suggested.
