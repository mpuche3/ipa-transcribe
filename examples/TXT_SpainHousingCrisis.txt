The Housing Crisis in Spain

On Friday, October 2, Spain's lower house of parliament rejected two emergency housing decrees, bringing the country's housing crisis into the news. The vote came after weeks of public anger and protests about rising rents and evictions. It was not a vote on whether housing is a problem; it was a clash over how far the government should go to protect tenants, and how those protections might affect the supply of homes.

One recent case made the debate painfully concrete. In Madrid, a woman aged 87 was evicted from the home where she had lived her entire life after her rent rose from 500 euros to 2,650 euros a month. The new rent was nearly twice her monthly pension of 1,350 euros. Public attention and protests followed, and she was later offered a provisional agreement to return. Her story is exceptional in its details, but it captures a common fear: that a home which once felt safe can be lost when rents rise faster than a family's income.

The problem is much larger than one family. Spain's central bank says the country is about 755,000 homes short of demand, and the gap could reach one million by 2028. In 2024, 28.1 percent of renters paying market rates spent more than 40 percent of their disposable income on housing, compared with 19.2 percent across the European Union. Figures like these explain why young adults struggle to leave their parents' homes and why working families fear each lease renewal. When housing costs absorb more income, people have less room to save, move for work, or cope with illness or job loss. A tight market turns a normal lease into a source of uncertainty.

The package rejected today tried to respond on several fronts. One decree would have extended protections through 2030 for vulnerable residents, made evictions harder, tightened rules on short-term rentals, discouraged speculative buying, and used tax incentives to encourage homes to be rented and built. A second would have renewed leases when they were due to end and required an owner who ended a contract to pay the tenant twelve months of rent. Supporters said the measures would give renters time and stability. Critics said tighter rules might push owners out of the rental market, leaving fewer homes to rent. Both concerns deserve attention: rules can protect people who already have a lease, but they are not enough on their own to create affordable homes.

That tension should not be used to dismiss either side. A tenant facing an abrupt eviction needs real protection, and a home should not become unaffordable simply because a contract is renewed. At the same time, lasting relief requires more homes that ordinary families can afford, faster construction where roads and services can support it, stable long-term rentals, and sensible limits on buying homes only for profit. The defeat leaves the government with a political problem as well as a housing one: how to find agreement without abandoning people under pressure. No single decree can solve years of rising costs and limited supply. But delay has a cost too. Spain's challenge is to make housing policy fair enough to protect renters and practical enough to expand the homes available to them.
