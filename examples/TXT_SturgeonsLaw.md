Sturgeon's Law
Sturgeon's law is the observation that ninety percent of everything is crud, and that the remaining ten percent is worth the search. The saying is named after Theodore Sturgeon, an American writer of science fiction who defended his field against critics during the 1950s. Readers who judged a whole genre by its worst examples, he argued, were repeating a mistake that applies to every human activity.
Sturgeon first set out the idea in print in 1957, in a book review column for the magazine Venture Science Fiction. He was preparing a positive notice of a novel by Philip K. Dick, and he wanted to answer the charge that science fiction was ninety percent trash. He conceded the point and then widened it. Cars, books, cheeses, hairstyles and people, he wrote, look like crud to an expert eye, apart from the acceptable share that each of us happens to enjoy.
He returned to the claim the following year with two corollaries. The first admits that a great deal of trash exists in science fiction, and adds that this is no more surprising than trash anywhere else. The second states that the best science fiction is as good as the best fiction in any other field. Together they move the argument away from percentages and toward a comparison of the finest work that each tradition produces.
The idea was not entirely new. Benjamin Disraeli wrote in 1870 that nine tenths of existing books are nonsense, and Rudyard Kipling suggested in 1891 that four fifths of everybody's work must be bad. George Orwell, reviewing books in 1946, claimed that in more than nine cases out of ten the only truthful criticism would be that a book is worthless. Sturgeon gave the thought a vivid shape, and his name became attached to it.
The law has since traveled well beyond fiction. The philosopher Daniel Dennett recommended it as a tool for critical thinking, noting that most experiments, poems and papers in every discipline are mediocre, and advising critics to study the best work rather than mock the worst. Engineers and programmers quote it whenever somebody judges a whole technology by its shabbiest examples. It is not a claim that quality is spread evenly, because the proportions are only a rough average, and nobody has tried to measure them.
The lasting value of Sturgeon's law lies in the habit of mind that it encourages. It warns against two errors at once: dismissing a genre because most of its products are poor, and defending a genre by pretending that its products are not. Judgments are best made from the best available examples, and a field with a low average may still contain work that nobody should ignore.
