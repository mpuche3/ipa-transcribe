Climbing Without a Rope
People assume I was born without fear, that something in my brain simply skipped the part that makes other people's hands shake on a ledge. That is not true, and it never was. As a kid I was quiet and awkward, more comfortable alone in a climbing gym than in a crowded room, and the first time I climbed something genuinely tall without a rope, my heart pounded so hard I could feel it in my teeth. Fear was never absent. What changed, slowly, over many years, was my relationship to it.
I started climbing indoors as a teenager, in a gym with padded floors and ropes clipped to every bolt, where falling meant nothing worse than a bruised ego. Indoor climbing let me fail constantly and safely, and something about that constant, low-stakes failure began to recalibrate what my body considered dangerous. When I eventually moved outside, onto real rock, I did the same thing there that I had done in the gym: I climbed the same routes over and over, with a rope, until my hands knew every hold without my mind having to search for it.
The turning point was not a single dramatic moment but thousands of small, deliberate repetitions. I would climb a route roped and protected until it felt as familiar as walking down my own hallway. Only then, once uncertainty had been replaced almost entirely by memory, would I consider climbing it again without protection. People who watched me free solo assumed they were watching an act of raw courage. What they were actually watching was the last visible step of a process that had mostly already happened, quietly, over dozens of earlier climbs nobody saw.
In 2017, I climbed El Capitan, a sheer granite wall in Yosemite nearly three thousand feet tall, without a rope, harness, or any other protection. If I had fallen at almost any point during those hours, I would have died. I had rehearsed that wall so many times, in sections and eventually as a whole, that by the time I actually climbed it without protection, my brain registered shockingly little fear. Brain scans taken later showed that my amygdala, the region tied to fear responses, simply produced less signal than most people's do when facing the same stimulus, and researchers believed years of deliberate exposure had helped shape that.
I do not think fearlessness is a gift some people receive and others do not. I think fear is a signal, and signals respond to information. The more familiar and rehearsed a situation becomes, the less urgently your body insists on warning you about it. That is not the absence of fear. It is fear that has finally run out of new information to react to.
