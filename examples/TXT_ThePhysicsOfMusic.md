The Physics of Music
Music reaches the ear as a pattern of pressure waves in air. A vibrating object, such as a plucked string or a struck drum, pushes and pulls the surrounding air, and those small changes in pressure travel outward at about 343 meters per second. When the waves arrive at the ear, they set the eardrum in motion, and the nervous system interprets that motion as sound. Everything we call music begins with this physical process.
The simplest wave is a pure tone with a single frequency, measured in hertz, which counts the cycles per second. Frequency determines pitch, and doubling the frequency raises the pitch by one octave. A note of 440 hertz, the standard tuning reference, has twice the frequency of a note of 220 hertz, and the two sound strikingly alike. Loudness, by contrast, depends on amplitude, the size of the pressure variation. Amplitude corresponds roughly to the energy the wave carries, although the response of the ear to it is logarithmic rather than linear.
A real instrument never produces a single frequency. A string fixed at both ends can vibrate in many patterns at once, and each pattern is a mode with its own frequency. The lowest mode gives the fundamental, and the higher modes give overtones whose frequencies are whole-number multiples of that fundamental. The mixture of these components, called the harmonic series, is what gives an instrument its timbre. A flute and a violin can play the same note at the same loudness and still sound completely different, because they emphasize different overtones.
Pitch also depends on the material and the geometry of the vibrating object. A shorter string, a tighter string, or a lighter string vibrates faster, which is why a guitarist presses a finger on the fretboard and turns a tuning peg. A tube of air behaves in a similar way, since a shorter column produces a higher note, and the pattern of standing waves inside the tube decides which frequencies are reinforced. The speed of sound in air rises slightly with temperature, so an instrument can drift out of tune in a cold room.
When two tones are played together, their waves add, and the result depends on the relationship between their frequencies. Simple proportions such as 2 to 1 or 3 to 2 produce a consonant sound, while complicated ones produce roughness. When two frequencies differ slightly, the combined amplitude rises and falls a few times each second, and a listener hears a slow pulse. Musicians exploit this effect when they tune by ear, because the beating stops only when the two tones match.
Physics explains how music works, but it does not explain why music moves us. The same wave that reaches the ear also reaches a brain that has learned to expect certain patterns, and expectation shapes the experience of tension and resolution. Understanding the mechanics of sound therefore leaves room for the listener, whose memory and culture turn a set of vibrations into a phrase, a melody, or a memory.
