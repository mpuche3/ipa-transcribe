Nuda Propiedad
Spain has a long tradition of films about the difficulty of finding a home, and Nuda Propiedad takes its place in it. Alberto Utrera directed the film from a screenplay he wrote with Carlos Soria, and it reached Spanish cinemas on 2 October 2026 with a running time of 99 minutes. Susana Abaitua and Maxi Iglesias play the couple at the center of the story, with David Lorente and Pepe Viyuela among the supporting cast. The title is a legal term, and the film treats it as a joke that slowly turns serious.
The arrangement the title names is the engine of the plot. Vera and Lucas are close to forty, they have rented for years, and they cannot see how they will ever buy a place of their own. Then they find a large apartment at half its market price. The reason for the discount is that they are buying only the bare ownership. The elderly man who lives there has sold the walls but kept the right to stay until he dies, which means that the couple owns the apartment and cannot use it. What looks like the answer to a decade of frustration becomes an obsession when the old man disappears, comes back in better health than before, and stops behaving like a man at the end of his life. Suspicion, frustration and an increasingly corrosive dark humor take over, and the couple begin to consider a plan that is as absurd as it is tempting.
Spanish civil law makes sense of the bargain. Full ownership can be divided into two parts, and each part can belong to a different person. The usufruct holder has the right to use the property and to take what it produces, often for life. The bare owner holds the title, may sell it and may leave it to an heir, but has no right of occupation while the usufruct lasts. The split appears most often in inheritance, when a surviving spouse keeps the use of the family home and the children receive the ownership without it. It has also become a financial product for elderly owners, who sell the walls to raise money and stay in the house. The buyer pays today for a benefit that begins on a day nobody can name, and that is why the arrangement suits a seller who is healthy and worries a buyer who is in a hurry.
The film understands that arithmetic, and most of its comedy comes from watching two decent people do it out loud. Every month of the old man's life is a month of their own, and the audience feels the calculation before the characters admit it. The cleverest move in the screenplay is that it makes us wait with them: each scene in which the old man seems healthy is a small disappointment for the viewer as well as for the couple, and the film lets us notice our own reaction. That discomfort is the point. The target is not the man in the apartment but the deal that put him there.
Critics have placed the film in the tradition of Spanish black comedy. One reviewer heard the accent of Azcona, the screenwriter who gave Spanish cinema its driest jokes about money and dignity, and another called the film an heir to El pisito, the 1959 comedy about a couple whose marriage depends on a death in the house where they rent. A third review described it as black comedy with suspense and a hint of paranoia, somewhere between Hitchcock and Woody Allen. Those comparisons set the bar high, and they also explain the disappointment of reviewers who wanted the film to be crueler than it is.
Utrera tempers the frenetic comic manner of his earlier features and gives his actors room to look at each other. Abaitua and Iglesias play a couple whose affection survives their calculations, and the film is careful to show that neither of them is a villain. The supporting players carry the farce, and the reviews singled out the physical transformation of the two leads, which makes their tiredness believable, and the comic timing of the older actors around them. Utrera also edited the film himself, and the patience of the cutting suits a story that is mostly about waiting.
The weaknesses are matters of degree rather than of construction. One critic complained that the film takes too long to set its conflict in motion, and another argued that it should be more caustic and ferocious, warning that its problems of rhythm will not improve with age. Both notes describe the same habit: the film is kinder than its premise, and it keeps offering its characters excuses that the audience stopped offering some time ago. The result is a comedy that is more comfortable than it is dangerous.
The reception was respectful rather than rapturous. Three of the main Spanish papers gave it three stars out of five, and the warmest notice praised its moments of delirious dark comedy while the coolest asked for more bite. Spanish film websites had it at 6.0 from a small number of votes, and it opened in a modest number of cinemas. That is a normal fate for a mid-budget Spanish comedy, and it may understate the film, because its subject has become the most reliable topic of conversation in the country.
The subject is the deal itself. A generation that was told to buy has found that the doors open to it carry conditions, and a home bought at half price with a stranger living inside it is a fair description of how many people now enter the market. The apartment in the film is not a home at first; it is a wager on somebody else's life expectancy. The couple is not wicked, and that is what makes them recognizable. They are people who did the sums that the market asked them to do.
Nuda propiedad means bare ownership, and the phrase does double duty. It describes a flat that can be owned but not used, and it describes a life in which the useful part has been handed to somebody else. The film is funniest when it keeps the two meanings in view, and weakest when it forgets that the man in the apartment has a claim on our sympathy too. A comedy about housing in Spain could be a great deal angrier, and this one chooses charm instead. That choice costs it some force, and it also produces the quality that its defenders noticed first: an elegant, light, politically incorrect pleasure in a situation that is not funny at all.
