A Home or an Asset? How Ideologies See the Housing Market
Oct 6, 2026 · @Miguel Puche
A house is two things at once. It is a place to live, as basic a need as food or clothing, and it is usually the most valuable asset a family will ever own. Almost every disagreement about housing policy comes from which of these two natures a person treats as primary.
Those who see the asset first tend to want a free market in which homes are bought, sold and invested in like shares or gold. Those who see the need first tend to want housing shielded from that market, with access treated as a right of citizenship. Between these poles sit several distinct traditions, each with its own account of what housing is for, who should own it and what the state should do about it.
Housing as an asset: the market liberal view
For market liberals and libertarians, a house is a commodity like any other, and the owner's right to buy, sell, rent or leave it empty is part of the basic right to property. Prices are treated as signals. When homes become expensive in a city, that is the market saying more should be built there, and investors who respond to that signal are doing something useful.
On this view, a housing crisis is almost always a supply crisis created by government. Zoning rules, height limits, slow permits and protected land stop builders from responding to demand, so prices rise. The remedy is to deregulate. Supporters point to Tokyo, where national rules make it comparatively easy to build and prices have stayed far more stable than in London or San Francisco. Rent control is rejected for the same reason: it is said to discourage construction and maintenance, and to reward whoever already holds a tenancy.
Critics answer that housing is not an ordinary commodity. Land in a given location cannot be manufactured, nobody can opt out of needing shelter, and supply takes years to respond. When homes are also treated as investments, they argue, speculative demand can push prices beyond what local wages will ever support.
The property-owning democracy: the conservative view
Conservatives also favour private ownership, but for social reasons more than economic ones. The argument is that people who own their homes have a stake in their neighbourhood and in the stability of the country. They look after what is theirs, they save, and they have something to pass on to their children. The phrase "property-owning democracy" was coined by the Scottish Conservative Noel Skelton in the 1920s and captures the ideal well.
The policies that follow aim to widen ownership, not simply to free the market. Margaret Thatcher's Right to Buy, introduced in 1980, let council tenants purchase their homes at large discounts, and around two million did so over the following decades. In Spain, Franco's housing minister José Luis Arrese put the same idea bluntly in the 1950s, saying the regime wanted a country of owners and not of proletarians. Subsidised mortgages and tax relief for buyers belong to this tradition too.
The weakness critics identify is that these policies help one generation at the expense of the next. Subsidising buyers tends to raise prices, existing owners gain a strong interest in blocking new construction, and the social homes that were sold were often never replaced.
A regulated mixed system: the social democratic view
Social democrats accept a private housing market but do not trust it to house everyone decently. Housing is treated as a social right, similar to healthcare or education, which the state must guarantee where the market fails. The goal is not to abolish private ownership but to place a large, good quality alternative beside it and to regulate the relationship between landlord and tenant.
Vienna is the model most often cited. Around half of its residents live in municipal or subsidised limited-profit housing, which is open to middle income households and not only to the poor. Because so many people have this option, private landlords must compete with it, which restrains rents across the whole city. Sweden's Million Programme, which built roughly a million homes between 1965 and 1974, and the large housing association sector in the Netherlands come from the same tradition. Rent regulation, secure tenancies and housing benefits complete the picture.
Opponents point to the costs. Regulated rents below market level produce queues, and in Stockholm the wait for a rent-regulated flat can last many years. Those inside the system are protected while newcomers and the young are left outside it, and public building programmes depend on sustained spending that governments often cut.
Housing outside the market: the socialist view
Socialists go a step further and argue that the problem is the market itself. As long as housing can be owned for profit, they say, landlords and investors will extract income from people who have no choice but to pay. Friedrich Engels made this case in The Housing Question in 1872, arguing that shortages were a permanent feature of capitalism and not an accident that reform could fix. The aim is therefore decommodification: homes should be allocated according to need, not according to who can pay most.
In practice this has taken very different forms. The Soviet Union and East Germany built and allocated housing through the state, which ended homelessness in the formal sense but produced long waiting lists, uniform design and little choice. More recent versions are less centralised and favour cooperatives, community land trusts and public ownership of large rental portfolios. In 2021 a majority of Berlin voters backed a referendum calling for the expropriation of the city's biggest corporate landlords, although it has not been implemented.
The standard objection is that removing prices removes the information and incentive that get homes built and maintained. Allocation by officials can also become allocation by connections, and expropriation discourages the private investment that most cities still rely on.
Private buildings, common land: the Georgist view
Georgism does not fit neatly on the line between left and right, because it splits the house from the ground beneath it. Henry George argued in Progress and Poverty in 1879 that a building is the product of someone's labour and capital and should belong to them without restriction. The land is different. Nobody made it, and its value comes almost entirely from what surrounds it: the transport links, schools, jobs and neighbours that the community as a whole has created.
From this follows the land value tax. The owner keeps the building and everything earned from improving it, but pays the community an annual charge based on the value of the site alone. Holding land idle while waiting for its price to rise becomes expensive, so speculation loses its appeal and owners are pushed to build or sell. The idea has admirers across the spectrum, including Milton Friedman, who called it the least bad tax. Estonia taxes land values, and Singapore applies a related logic: the state owns most of the land and roughly four in five residents live in public flats held on 99 year leases.
The practical difficulties are valuing land separately from buildings and the political cost of taxing owners whose homes are valuable but whose incomes are modest.
What the disagreement is really about
These traditions disagree less about facts than about three underlying questions. The first is what a house is for: an investment, a foundation for family and community, or a basic need. The second is who should capture the rise in land values, whether the individual owner, the state or the community. The third is whom to trust with allocation, the price mechanism or public institutions.
Few countries give a single answer. Spain is a good illustration. Its constitution recognises a right to decent and adequate housing, and a 2023 law allows rent caps in areas declared to be under market stress. Yet about three quarters of households own their homes, the social rental stock is among the smallest in Western Europe, and property remains a favoured form of saving. The asset and the right coexist in the same system and pull against each other.
That tension is unlikely to disappear, because it reflects something true about housing itself. A policy that protects the value of existing homes makes them harder for newcomers to afford, and a policy that makes homes cheap reduces the wealth of those who already own. Each ideology is, in the end, a decision about which of those two groups to favour and how much.
