Loss Aversion
Loss aversion is the tendency to weigh losses more heavily than gains of the same size. In experiments, people often reject a bet that gives an equal chance of winning or losing a modest sum, because the pain of the possible loss outweighs the pleasure of the possible gain. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky introduced the idea in 1979 as a central part of prospect theory, and it has since become one of the most widely cited findings in behavioral economics.
Prospect theory describes how people actually choose, rather than how a perfectly rational agent would choose. Kahneman and Tversky observed that people judge outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually their present situation, and that the curve relating value to change falls more steeply on the loss side than on the gain side. Studies of the value function put the gap at roughly two to one: a loss of a given size hurts about twice as much as a gain of the same size pleases. The steep slope of that curve is what the term loss aversion names.
The same reluctance helps to explain the endowment effect, the tendency of people to demand more money to give up an object than they would pay to acquire it. Once a person owns a mug, a ticket, or a bottle of wine, handing it over is coded as a loss, while buying it is coded as a gain. Because losses loom larger, the price that an owner names exceeds the price that a buyer would offer for the identical item.
A related pattern appears whenever a choice is framed as a loss or as a gain. Doctors who read that a treatment would save a certain number of patients tend to recommend it, while doctors who read that the same treatment would let the rest die tend to reject it. Advertising, tax policy, and political campaigns exploit the same asymmetry, because a warning about a loss moves people more than a promise of an equal gain.
Loss aversion may also lie behind the status quo bias, the preference for keeping things as they are. Every change brings both gains and losses, and the losses receive more weight in the decision. This reasoning has been used to explain why investors hold losing stocks too long and sell winning stocks too early, and why workers hesitate to switch to a health plan that is better on average but different from the one they already have.
The effect is not equally strong for everyone or in every setting. It appears most clearly when the stakes are modest and the outcome is judged on its own, and it weakens when a person weighs many decisions together or when a loss can be seen as the cost of a larger goal. Traders and experienced gamblers, who read their results over long sequences of bets, are less troubled by a single loss than beginners are.
Some researchers have questioned how large the effect really is. When critics repeated the original experiments with more participants and tighter controls, several of the classic demonstrations produced smaller differences than the first reports had suggested, and a few failed to appear at all. Defenders reply that the underlying asymmetry survives these corrections, even though the published figures were probably too generous.
In summary, loss aversion describes a genuine and well documented feature of human choice: people do not treat a loss and a gain of the same size as mirror images. The principle remains useful to anyone who must predict how people will respond to a new price, a new policy, or a new risk, and it explains a wide range of behavior that a model of pure self interest struggles to account for.
