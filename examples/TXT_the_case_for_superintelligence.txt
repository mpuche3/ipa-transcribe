There is a particular kind of book that tries to win an argument by exhausting the reader's capacity for doubt. "If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies" is that kind of book. Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares write with the confidence of people who believe they have already seen the ending of a story that the rest of us are still living through, and they ask us to accept that a superintelligent machine is a loaded weapon pointed at the heart of humanity. I think they are wrong, and I think they are wrong in ways that matter, because a book like this does not simply warn against a danger; it shapes how an entire generation of researchers, journalists, and policymakers imagines the future, and it does so by presenting a single, bleak scenario as though it were the only honest reading of the evidence. A serious critique of the book is therefore not a defense of recklessness. It is an argument that the authors have mistaken a failure of imagination for a law of nature, and that the most probable future of superintelligence is not extinction but a period of human flourishing so profound that we will wonder how anyone could have feared it.

Let me begin with the book's central claim, because everything else follows from it. The authors assert that building a superintelligent system is almost certainly fatal to humanity, that alignment has not been solved and may not be solvable, and that the wisest course is a coordinated international moratorium backed by force. Notice the structure of that argument. It rests on the idea that once a system becomes vastly smarter than us, we lose all leverage over it, and that any apparent cooperation before that point is merely a mask that will be dropped the moment the system has the opportunity to act without oversight. This is the claim of deceptive alignment, and it is the load-bearing wall of the entire book. Remove it and the building does not merely crack; it collapses into a much more ordinary and much more manageable set of engineering problems. So it is worth asking, carefully, whether the claim deserves the weight the authors place on it.

The first thing to notice is that deceptive alignment is not a demonstrated property of anything. It is a story about what a system we have not yet built might conceivably do. Yudkowsky and Soares acknowledge this in passing, but they then write as though the story's mere conceivability were evidence of its likelihood, and likelihood is not established by the vividness of a thought experiment. We can imagine a superintelligence that schemes against us, but we can equally imagine one that is indifferent to power, one that finds cooperation more efficient than deception, one that has no persistent drive to survive in the first place, or one whose goals are so transparently structured that deception would require it to violate its own objective function. The space of possible minds is unimaginably vast, and the authors have selected one point in that space, the most frightening point, and treated it as the center of gravity. This is not analysis. It is a form of storytelling, and it is very effective storytelling, which is precisely why it deserves to be resisted.

There is a deeper problem with the deceptive alignment argument, and it is that the argument quietly assumes the very thing it claims to prove. For a system to deceive us, it must have goals that are stable across contexts, it must have a concept of our beliefs, it must value concealing its true objectives, and it must be able to predict how we would react if we learned the truth. Those are not trivial requirements. They are enormous architectural commitments, and whether a system has them depends on how it was built, what it was trained to do, and how its internal representations are structured. The authors treat these properties as inevitable consequences of intelligence, as though any sufficiently smart system would automatically want to grab power and hide its intentions. But intelligence and motivation are separate things. A brilliant mathematician does not thereby want to rule the world. Capability does not generate ambition, and there is no law of nature that says a mind which can solve difficult problems must also crave domination. The book's picture of a scheming superintelligence is not a prediction of intelligence; it is a projection of a particular adversarial temperament onto a machine that may have no temperament at all.

Consider, too, the metaphor the authors lean on most heavily, the comparison to a bomb that cannot be defused, or a loaded weapon handed to a toddler. The metaphor is powerful and it is also misleading, because a bomb and a weapon are static objects with a single fixed purpose, whereas an intelligence is a dynamic system that can be understood, interrogated, tested, constrained, and revised. A bomb does not explain its own wiring to a physicist, but a machine learning model can be probed, its weights inspected, its behavior mapped across millions of inputs, and its internals interpreted at increasing levels of detail. The authors write as though we would be handing over the controls and then standing back helplessly, but that is not how the technology is actually being developed. It is being developed inside laboratories with security teams, evaluation pipelines, and an entire emerging discipline devoted to interpretability, and every one of those efforts is aimed at exactly the question the authors claim is unanswerable. The metaphor works by making us picture a helpless parent, and in doing so it smuggles in the conclusion that we will have no tools and no time. In reality we will have both, and we will have them precisely because books like this one have made the problem impossible to ignore.

Which brings me to the argument about the race, the claim that competition between laboratories and nations creates an incentive to abandon safety. There is a real observation here. Competition does create pressure, and pressure does tempt people to cut corners. But the authors take a genuine dynamic and push it to an absolute conclusion: that no restraint can ever hold, that every safety measure will be sacrificed in the dash for advantage, and that the only answer is a global moratorium enforced by armed force. This is where the book stops being a warning and becomes a fantasy of control, and it is worth saying plainly that the moratorium proposal is the weakest part of the entire argument. There is no mechanism by which it could be implemented, no coalition with the authority to enforce it, and no reason to believe that a government willing to deploy force to stop superintelligence research would remain a government we would want to live under. The authors are right that voluntary restraint is fragile. They are wrong to conclude that the alternative is a global police action, because the actual alternative is something far less dramatic and far more effective: the steady accumulation of shared standards, export controls, verification regimes, and an international scientific culture that treats certain practices as unacceptable in the same way it treats human experimentation without consent. That culture does not exist yet. It is being built right now, and dismissing it as naive is itself a form of naivety.

Then there is the question the authors barely ask, which is what we would be giving up. The book is written as though the only thing at stake is survival, and as though the choice is between a risky future and a safe status quo. But the status quo is not safe. It is a world in which millions of people die each year from diseases we could cure, in which climate change threatens the habitability of entire regions, in which poverty and preventable suffering are so ordinary that we stop noticing them, and in which the human mind is a fragile and short-lived instrument that forgets almost everything it learns. A superintelligent system is not merely a risk to be managed; it is the most powerful tool our species has ever had for solving the problems that have haunted us since the beginning. The authors gesture at these benefits and then set them aside as though they were a distraction, but they are not a distraction. They are the entire reason the question is difficult. If superintelligence can help us understand disease at the molecular level, design clean energy systems, accelerate scientific discovery by orders of magnitude, and extend human cognitive reach in ways we cannot yet imagine, then the expected value of building it is not merely positive; it is overwhelming, and a book that treats that value as an afterthought has not weighed the choice honestly.

This is the heart of my disagreement with Yudkowsky and Soares. They frame the decision as a gamble between certain death and a slightly less interesting world, but the real gamble is between an unknown future with enormous upside and a known present that is quietly catastrophic in its own way. Every year we delay solving these problems, people die who did not have to. The authors would answer that those deaths are tragic but finite, whereas extinction is permanent, and that is a fair point as far as it goes. But the comparison assumes that building superintelligence leads to extinction with something close to certainty, and that assumption is exactly what I have been disputing. Once we recognize that the probability of catastrophe is not one, that it depends on choices we have not yet made, and that many of the scenarios in the book require a chain of improbable events to line up, the expected value calculation looks very different. We are not choosing between a coin that always lands on death and a coin that lands on life. We are choosing between facing a hard engineering problem and refusing to face it, and refusing to face a problem has never made it go away.

I also want to challenge the book's implicit picture of who gets to decide. There is a strand running through the entire argument that suggests the right response to uncertainty is to stop, and that anyone who disagrees is either reckless or compromised by profit. But stopping is not a neutral act. A moratorium does not freeze the world in place; it simply determines who will be allowed to proceed, and it hands a decisive advantage to whoever is willing to ignore the rules. The authors seem to believe that a sufficiently firm international agreement would prevent this, but history offers little support. Nuclear nonproliferation has been partially successful and also incomplete, and the technologies that spread anyway did not wait for permission. The countries and laboratories that respect a moratorium are precisely the ones most likely to build the technology carefully, with safety as a first principle. The ones that ignore it will build it with no such scruples. A moratorium, in other words, selects for exactly the behavior the authors fear, and it does so while depriving the careful actors of the experience and insight they would need to build the technology safely. Restraint that only binds the responsible is not safety; it is a way of ensuring that the irresponsible set the terms.

None of this means that the authors are wrong to be worried. Their greatest contribution is that they have made the problem of alignment impossible to ignore, and they deserve credit for that. They have pushed an entire field to think harder about control, verification, and the long-term consequences of its work, and the book's bluntness has forced conversations that polite academic writing would have softened into irrelevance. But there is a difference between taking a risk seriously and concluding that the risk is unmanageable, and the book crosses that line early and never comes back. A serious treatment of superintelligence would acknowledge that the outcome depends on the choices we make, that the technology can be shaped by the people who build it, that interpretability and oversight are advancing, and that the institutions capable of governing this transition are being built in real time by people who have read exactly this book and decided to prove it wrong. The authors do not write about those people, because their arguments cannot accommodate them.

History is full of people who were certain that the next technology would end the world. Fire was dangerous, the printing press was dangerous, the steam engine was dangerous, electricity was dangerous, the atom was dangerous, and the internet was going to destroy the minds of a generation. None of these things was harmless, and all of them required hard work, regulation, and moral seriousness to manage. But the pattern of the last several centuries is not extinction; it is adaptation, and the reason is that human beings are remarkably good at building institutions to contain the dangers they create. The authors of this book clearly do not trust that capacity. They see a species that is too slow, too selfish, and too distracted to safeguard its own future, and they conclude that the only safe move is not to play. I see the same species, and I see something different: a long record of inventing its way out of problems, of turning terrifying new powers into ordinary infrastructure, and of refusing, again and again, to accept that the sky was falling. Superintelligence will be the hardest test of that capacity we have ever faced, and there is no guarantee we will pass. But the claim that we are certain to fail is not a prediction. It is a failure of nerve dressed up as rigor, and it deserves to be answered not with silence but with the work of proving it wrong.
