Prospect Theory: An Essay
Prospect theory is the most influential account we have of how people actually choose when outcomes are uncertain, as opposed to how a perfectly rational agent ought to choose. It was proposed by the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in a paper published in the journal Econometrica in 1979, under the title "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk", and it went on to become one of the most cited papers in the history of economics. Its central claims are simple to state. People evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than as final states of wealth. Losses hurt more than equivalent gains please. Sensitivity to both gains and losses diminishes as they grow larger. And people do not treat probabilities at face value, but give too much weight to unlikely events and too little to likely ones. From these few ingredients follows a remarkably rich picture of human behaviour, one that explains why the same person buys both insurance and lottery tickets, why investors cling to losing shares, why the wording of a medical choice can reverse a doctor's recommendation, and why people demand far more to give up an object than they would ever pay to acquire it.
To appreciate what prospect theory changed, it helps to understand what came before it. The story begins in the eighteenth century with a puzzle known as the St Petersburg paradox. Imagine a game in which a coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the prize doubles with every tail that precedes that first head. The mathematical expected value of this game is infinite, yet nobody would pay more than a modest sum to play it. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli offered a resolution: people do not value money in proportion to its amount, but according to the satisfaction, or utility, that it brings, and each additional unit of wealth brings a little less satisfaction than the one before. A thousand ducats mean a great deal to a pauper and very little to a rich merchant. If utility grows with the logarithm of wealth, the St Petersburg game is worth only a finite and rather small amount, and the paradox dissolves. Bernoulli's idea also explained risk aversion in general. If the utility curve is concave, then the utility of a sure amount is greater than the average utility of a gamble with the same expected value, and so a sensible person prefers the sure thing.
Two centuries later John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern gave this idea a rigorous foundation. In their 1944 book on the theory of games they showed that if a person's preferences among gambles satisfy a small set of apparently innocent axioms, then that person behaves as if maximising the expected value of some utility function. The axioms ask that preferences be complete and transitive, that they be continuous, and that they obey a principle called independence, which says roughly that if you prefer one gamble to another, mixing both with the same third gamble should not change your preference. Expected utility theory, as the result came to be called, was elegant, general and normatively compelling. It quickly became the foundation of the economics of uncertainty, of finance, of insurance theory and of statistical decision theory. Crucially, economists came to treat it not only as a standard for how one should choose, but also as a description of how people do choose.
Cracks appeared almost immediately. In 1953 the French economist Maurice Allais presented a pair of choices to a gathering of the leading decision theorists of the day, several of whom promptly violated the independence axiom they had helped to defend. The Allais paradox shows that people place a special premium on certainty: an option that guarantees a good outcome is far more attractive than one that merely makes it very probable, and this preference for certainty leads to patterns of choice that no utility function can rationalise. A decade later Daniel Ellsberg showed that people also prefer known risks to unknown ones in ways the theory forbids. Harry Markowitz, in a short paper of 1952, noticed something else that sat awkwardly with Bernoulli. People at every level of wealth seem to be averse to small symmetric bets and yet attracted to long shots, which suggests that utility is attached to changes in wealth, to gains and losses from wherever one currently stands, rather than to total wealth. Markowitz sketched a utility curve with a kink at the current position, but did not develop the idea into a full theory.
These anomalies were known, but for a long time they were regarded as curiosities, perhaps mistakes that people would correct if the error were explained to them. What Kahneman and Tversky did in the 1970s was to treat such departures as data about the mind. They had already spent several years studying judgement, documenting the mental shortcuts by which people estimate probabilities and the systematic biases that those shortcuts produce. Turning from judgement to choice, they adopted a deliberately simple method. They wrote short hypothetical problems, each a choice between two gambles, gave them to students and university staff in Israel, Sweden and the United States, and looked for cases where a large majority chose in a way that expected utility theory ruled out. They were not looking for noise or confusion. They were looking for regularities, for errors that were systematic and that the two authors could feel in their own intuitions. The result was a catalogue of effects and a theory designed to account for all of them at once.
The first effect in the catalogue was the certainty effect, a refinement of what Allais had found. Asked to choose between a gamble offering 2,500 with probability 0.33, 2,400 with probability 0.66 and nothing with probability 0.01, and a sure 2,400, about four fifths of respondents took the sure amount. Asked instead to choose between 2,500 with probability 0.33 and 2,400 with probability 0.34, about the same proportion took the first option. The second pair is obtained from the first simply by removing a 0.66 chance of winning 2,400 from both options, so expected utility theory requires the same preference in both cases. People reverse because removing that common component turns a certainty into a mere probability on one side, while on the other side it only turns one probability into a smaller one. The reduction from certain to probable is felt far more keenly than a reduction of the same size in the middle of the range.
The second effect, which Kahneman and Tversky called the reflection effect, was more novel. When they took their problems and replaced every gain with a loss of the same size, preferences flipped like an image in a mirror. Offered a choice between an 80 percent chance of winning 4,000 and a sure 3,000, four out of five people took the sure gain. Offered a choice between an 80 percent chance of losing 4,000 and a sure loss of 3,000, more than nine out of ten took the gamble. People are risk averse when choosing among gains and risk seeking when choosing among losses. This is hard to square with Bernoulli. A concave utility function over total wealth predicts caution everywhere, and it certainly does not predict that the appetite for risk should depend on whether the same final positions are described as gains or as losses.
A third effect, the isolation effect, showed that people simplify choices by ignoring the components that the options share and focusing on what distinguishes them. Because a given pair of options can be broken into shared and distinctive components in more than one way, different presentations of the same choice can produce different preferences. In one demonstration, people were first told they had been given 1,000 and then asked to choose between a coin flip for another 1,000 and a sure 500. Most took the sure 500. Others were told they had been given 2,000 and asked to choose between a coin flip to lose 1,000 and a sure loss of 500. Most took the gamble. In terms of final wealth the two problems are identical. The initial bonus, being common to both options, was simply left out of the evaluation, and what remained was coded as a gain in one version and a loss in the other.
From these observations Kahneman and Tversky drew the conclusion that defines their theory: the carriers of value are changes in wealth, not states of wealth. This is the principle of reference dependence. It is rooted in a general feature of perception. Our senses respond to contrasts and changes rather than to absolute magnitudes. A room feels bright or dim depending on what the eye has adapted to, and water at a given temperature feels warm to one hand and cold to the other if the hands have been resting in cold and hot basins. In the same way, an outcome is experienced as good or bad relative to some neutral reference point, which is usually the status quo but can also be an expectation, an aspiration, a goal, or the outcome that someone else received. Two people who end the day with the same fortune will feel very differently about it if one began the day richer and the other poorer. Kahneman later observed that this simple fact, obvious to any psychologist, had been invisible in Bernoulli's framework for more than two hundred years, a blindness he attributed to the difficulty of noticing flaws in a theory one has accepted and used.
The theory represents this idea with a value function, which replaces the utility function of the older theory. The value function is defined over gains and losses and has three essential properties. First, it passes through the reference point, which is assigned a value of zero. Second, it shows diminishing sensitivity in both directions. The difference between gaining 100 and gaining 200 feels larger than the difference between gaining 1,100 and gaining 1,200, and the same holds for losses. This makes the function concave for gains and convex for losses, which gives it a characteristic S shape and directly produces the reflection effect. If the second thousand gained adds less value than the first, a sure gain is worth more than a gamble with the same expected value. If the second thousand lost adds less pain than the first, a gamble that offers a chance of escaping loss altogether is less painful than a sure loss of the same expected size.
The third property is the most famous. The value function is steeper for losses than for gains, so that it has a kink at the reference point. This is loss aversion. Most people refuse a bet on a fair coin in which they stand to lose 100 unless the possible gain is around 200 or more. The pain of losing a given amount is psychologically about twice as powerful as the pleasure of gaining it. Kahneman and Tversky suggested that this asymmetry has evolutionary roots: an organism that treats threats as more urgent than opportunities has a better chance of surviving to reproduce. Whatever its origin, loss aversion explains a kind of risk aversion that the Bernoullian theory handles badly, namely aversion to small gambles. The economist Matthew Rabin later proved that if a concave utility of wealth were the only source of risk aversion, then a person who turns down a coin flip to lose 100 or win 110 at all wealth levels would also have to turn down a coin flip to lose 1,000 or win any sum whatsoever, which is absurd. Reluctance to take small favourable bets cannot come from the curvature of utility over lifetime wealth. It comes from the kink.
Loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity pull in different directions when a choice involves both gains and losses, and the balance between them determines behaviour. In mixed gambles, where one can either win or lose, loss aversion dominates and people are cautious. In choices where all outcomes are losses, diminishing sensitivity dominates and people gamble. This is why someone facing a bad situation with no good options will often take a desperate risk rather than accept a certain loss. Kahneman and Tversky noted that a person who has not made peace with his losses is likely to accept gambles that would be unacceptable to him otherwise. The gambler who doubles his stakes late in the evening, the firm that pours money into a failing project, and the general who commits his reserves to a battle already lost are all behaving as the value function predicts.
The second pillar of the theory concerns probability. In expected utility theory the utility of each outcome is multiplied by its probability, so that probabilities enter the calculation linearly. A move from a 5 percent chance to a 10 percent chance matters exactly as much as a move from 50 to 55 or from 95 to 100. Psychologically this is plainly false. The step from impossibility to possibility, from zero to 5 percent, creates hope where there was none, and people will pay a great deal for it. The step from 95 to 100 percent removes a nagging doubt, and people will pay a great deal for that too. The step from 50 to 55 percent is barely noticed. Prospect theory captures this by multiplying the value of each outcome not by its probability but by a decision weight, a transformation of the probability that reflects its impact on choice.
The weighting function has a distinctive shape. Small probabilities are overweighted, so that a one in a thousand chance receives more than a thousandth of the weight that certainty would receive. Moderate and high probabilities are underweighted, so that a 90 percent chance receives noticeably less than nine tenths of the weight of certainty. Near the two ends the function changes abruptly, reflecting the special status of the impossible and the certain. Kahneman and Tversky were careful to say that decision weights are not beliefs. A person may know perfectly well that the chance is one in a thousand and still give that chance a disproportionate place in the decision. The distortion lies in how the probability is used, not in how it is estimated.
Combining the weighting function with the value function yields what has come to be called the fourfold pattern of risk attitudes, which is perhaps the most distinctive prediction of the theory. For gains of high probability, people are risk averse. They will accept a settlement well below the expected value of a strong legal claim in order to lock in a sure result, because the remaining chance of getting nothing looms large. For gains of low probability, people are risk seeking. They buy lottery tickets at prices far above expected value, because the tiny chance of a fortune is overweighted. For losses of low probability, people are risk averse. They buy insurance and extended warranties at prices above expected cost, because the small chance of disaster is overweighted. And for losses of high probability, people are risk seeking. Facing an almost certain large loss, they reject a slightly smaller sure loss and gamble on the slim chance of getting away with nothing. This last cell, where diminishing sensitivity to losses is reinforced by the underweighting of near certainty, is the one in which bad situations are turned into catastrophes. Expected utility theory with a concave utility function predicts only one of these four attitudes without strain. Prospect theory predicts all of them from two curves.
The 1979 theory also described a stage that comes before evaluation, which the authors called editing. Before weighing a prospect, a person organises and simplifies it. Outcomes are coded as gains or losses relative to a reference point. Probabilities attached to identical outcomes are combined. A riskless component is separated from the risky one. Components shared by the options under comparison are cancelled. Numbers are rounded and extremely unlikely outcomes may be dropped. Options that are obviously dominated are detected and discarded. The editing stage was the least formal part of the theory, and many later treatments ignore it, but it carried an important message: preferences depend on how a problem is mentally represented, and the same problem can be represented in more than one way.
This message was developed in a paper of 1981 on what Tversky and Kahneman called framing. Their best known example is the Asian disease problem. Respondents were asked to imagine that the country was preparing for the outbreak of an unusual disease expected to kill 600 people, and that two programmes had been proposed. In one version, the first programme would save 200 people, and the second offered a one third chance of saving all 600 and a two thirds chance of saving nobody. Nearly three quarters of respondents chose the first programme, the sure saving of 200 lives. In another version, under the first programme 400 people would die, and under the second there was a one third chance that nobody would die and a two thirds chance that all 600 would die. Now more than three quarters chose the second programme, the gamble. The two versions describe exactly the same outcomes. The only difference is that the first describes them as lives saved, with an implicit reference point in which everybody dies, and the second describes them as lives lost, with a reference point in which nobody dies. Risk aversion for gains and risk seeking for losses did the rest.
Framing effects are troubling in a way that ordinary risk attitudes are not. One can argue that loss aversion is a matter of taste, and that a person who feels losses more keenly than gains is entitled to choose accordingly. One cannot argue that a rational person should prefer one programme when the outcomes are counted as survivors and another when the very same outcomes are counted as deaths. Framing violates the principle of invariance, the requirement that preferences should not depend on the description of the options, which is so basic to rational choice that it is rarely even stated as an axiom. Similar reversals have been found among experienced professionals. In one study physicians were more willing to recommend surgery over radiation for lung cancer when the outcomes of surgery were stated as a 90 percent chance of surviving the first month than when they were stated as a 10 percent chance of dying in it. The lesson Kahneman and Tversky drew was sobering. People generally accept a decision problem in the form in which it is presented to them and seldom reframe it for themselves, so those who control the presentation have considerable power over the choice.
The original theory had technical weaknesses, and its authors knew it. Because each probability was transformed separately, the theory could in some cases predict that a person would choose a gamble that was worse in every respect than an available alternative, a violation of what is called stochastic dominance. The editing stage was supposed to remove such cases, but this was a patch rather than a solution. The original theory was also limited to gambles with at most two outcomes other than zero, and it applied only to risk, where probabilities are stated, and not to uncertainty, where they are not. In 1992 Tversky and Kahneman published a revised version, cumulative prospect theory, which solved these problems by borrowing an idea developed in the 1980s by John Quiggin and others. Instead of transforming each probability on its own, the new version transforms cumulative probabilities, that is, the probability of getting an outcome at least as good as a given one for gains, or at least as bad for losses. The decision weight of an outcome then depends on its rank among the possible outcomes. The practical effect is that it is the extreme outcomes, the best and the worst, that are overweighted when they are unlikely, not every unlikely outcome regardless of its position.
The 1992 paper also put numbers on the theory. Using choices made by a group of graduate students, Tversky and Kahneman estimated a value function in which gains and losses are each raised to a power of about 0.88, reflecting mild diminishing sensitivity, and in which losses are multiplied by a coefficient of about 2.25, reflecting loss aversion. They estimated a weighting function with an inverse S shape that crosses the diagonal at a probability of roughly one third, so that chances below that level are overweighted and chances above it are underweighted. These estimates have been used in thousands of later studies. They should not be read as constants of nature. Later work finds a good deal of variation between individuals, between cultures and between kinds of stakes, and a recent meta analysis of several hundred estimates puts the typical loss aversion coefficient a little below two. But the qualitative shape has held up well, and the figure of roughly two to one has become part of the common vocabulary of social science.
Reference dependence and loss aversion turned out to matter well beyond choices among gambles. In 1980 Richard Thaler, then a young economist collecting examples of behaviour his colleagues could not explain, described what he named the endowment effect. People often demand much more to give up an object they own than they would be willing to pay to obtain it. A man who bought a case of wine for a modest price years ago would neither sell a bottle at its present high market price nor buy another at that price. In the best known experiment, conducted by Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Thaler, half the students in a classroom were given coffee mugs and a market was opened. Since the mugs had been handed out at random, standard theory predicts that about half of them should change hands. In fact very few did. Owners typically asked about twice as much to part with a mug as buyers were prepared to pay. The explanation is that giving up the mug is coded as a loss and acquiring it as a gain, and losses loom larger. Ownership moves the reference point.
The same mechanism generates status quo bias, documented by William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser in 1988. When one option is designated as the current state of affairs, people choose it far more often than when the same option is presented neutrally, because the disadvantages of switching are weighted more heavily than the advantages. This helps to explain why employees stay with whatever pension plan they are first enrolled in, why consumers keep their electricity supplier and their bank, and why default options have such a strong influence in matters ranging from organ donation to software settings. It also explains much of the difficulty of reform. Any change to an institution creates winners and losers, and the losers will fight harder than the winners, since what they stand to lose weighs more with them than the equivalent gain weighs with the others.
Thaler extended these ideas into a broader account that he called mental accounting. People do not treat money as perfectly interchangeable. They assign it to separate mental accounts, such as housekeeping, holidays, savings and windfalls, and they evaluate gains and losses within each account rather than across their wealth as a whole. Combined with the S shaped value function, this yields predictions about how people prefer outcomes to be packaged. Because of diminishing sensitivity, two separate gains bring more pleasure than a single gain of the same total, while two separate losses bring more pain than a single combined loss. People would therefore rather have their gains segregated and their losses integrated, which is why sellers list a product's benefits one by one and bundle its charges into a single price, and why a small extra cost is readily accepted when it is attached to a large purchase. Mental accounting also explains the sunk cost fallacy. An account opened with a purchase is closed in the red if the purchase is wasted, and people will go to considerable lengths, such as driving through a snowstorm to a match they no longer wish to see, to avoid closing an account at a loss.
A related finding is that prior outcomes alter the appetite for risk. Thaler and Eric Johnson showed that after a gain people become more willing to gamble, as if betting with the house's money, because subsequent losses can be absorbed into the earlier gain without crossing the reference point. After a loss, people tend to become more cautious in general, yet are strongly attracted to gambles that offer a chance to break even. The reference point, in other words, does not always adjust at once. When it lags behind events, a person may be sitting in the domain of gains or of losses when a new choice arrives, and will choose accordingly. This makes the reference point at once the most powerful element of the theory and the most elusive, a point to which the critics have often returned.
A theory built on hypothetical questions put to students would be of limited interest if it did not describe behaviour where real money is at stake. Much of the work of the past four decades has consisted of taking prospect theory out of the laboratory, and finance has been the most fertile ground. In 1985 Hersh Shefrin and Meir Statman described the disposition effect, the tendency of investors to sell shares that have risen since purchase and to hold on to shares that have fallen. Terrance Odean later confirmed the effect in the trading records of thousands of customers of a brokerage firm, and showed that it was costly, since the winners that investors sold went on to outperform the losers they kept, and since the tax system rewards doing precisely the opposite. The purchase price acts as a reference point. A share standing above it sits in the domain of gains, where people are risk averse and inclined to take the sure profit. A share standing below it sits in the domain of losses, where people are risk seeking and prefer to hold on in the hope of getting back to even.
Prospect theory has also been offered as an answer to one of the great puzzles of financial economics. Over the long run shares have returned far more than government bonds, by a margin so wide that it can be reconciled with standard theory only by assuming an implausibly extreme aversion to risk. Shlomo Benartzi and Thaler proposed in 1995 that the answer lies in the combination of loss aversion with frequent evaluation, a combination they called myopic loss aversion. Shares fall in value over short periods far more often than over long ones. An investor who checks a portfolio every few months and feels each decline about twice as sharply as each rise of the same size will find shares painful to hold and will demand a large premium for holding them. Benartzi and Thaler calculated that the observed premium is consistent with investors who evaluate their holdings roughly once a year. Experiments have since shown that people who are shown their returns less often do in fact invest more in risky assets. The probability weighting side of the theory has found its own applications in finance. Nicholas Barberis and Ming Huang showed that investors who overweight small chances of large gains will overpay for securities with lottery like returns, which helps to explain the poor average performance of newly listed shares, of the shares of distressed companies and of options far from the current price.
Insurance offers a second field of evidence. Households regularly choose low deductibles on home and motor policies at a price that could be justified only if their risk of a claim were several times higher than it actually is, and they buy extended warranties on appliances at prices well above the expected cost of repair. Studies of large samples of insurance choices find that overweighting of small probabilities explains these patterns better than risk aversion of the ordinary kind. At the same time, people often fail to insure against rare catastrophes such as floods and earthquakes unless a disaster has recently occurred. This apparent contradiction points to a complication in the theory that Kahneman and Tversky recognised from the start: very small probabilities are either overweighted or neglected altogether, depending on whether the possibility has been brought vividly to mind.
In labour markets, loss aversion relative to a target appears in several well known studies. Colin Camerer and his colleagues found that New York taxi drivers who rented their cabs by the day seemed to work until they reached a daily income target and then go home, so that they worked shorter hours on busy days, when earnings per hour were high, and longer hours on slow days, the reverse of what standard theory recommends. This finding has been disputed, and later studies with richer data suggest that the effect is smaller than first reported and fades with experience, but targets do appear to play some role. Workers resist cuts in their nominal wages far more fiercely than they resist an equal loss of purchasing power through inflation, which is a reason why wages tend to be rigid downward and why recessions produce unemployment rather than lower pay. Experiments with incentive pay have found that bonuses given in advance, to be returned if targets are missed, can raise effort more than the same bonuses promised as rewards.
The housing market shows the same pattern. David Genesove and Christopher Mayer studied apartment sellers in Boston during a slump in the 1990s and found that owners whose flats had fallen in value since purchase set higher asking prices than otherwise similar sellers, waited longer for a buyer, and often did obtain somewhat higher prices when they finally sold. The original purchase price, which is economically irrelevant to what a property is now worth, served as a reference point below which a sale registered as a loss. A particularly elegant test comes from professional golf. Devin Pope and Maurice Schweitzer analysed millions of putts on the leading tour and found that players were more accurate when putting to avoid a score worse than par than when putting from the same distance for a score better than par. Par has no bearing on the result of a tournament, in which only the total number of strokes counts, yet even the best players in the world, with large sums at stake, try a little harder to avoid the loss than to secure the gain.
The theory has also changed how policy is designed. If defaults are powerful because departing from them is felt as a loss, then the choice of default deserves care. Automatic enrolment in pension schemes, with freedom to opt out, has raised participation dramatically wherever it has been introduced. A programme devised by Thaler and Benartzi invites employees to commit in advance to saving a share of their future pay rises, so that the higher contribution never shows up as a cut in take home pay and loss aversion is never triggered. Messages about health are sometimes more persuasive when they stress what will be lost by inaction than what will be gained by action, and charges, such as a small fee for a plastic bag, often change behaviour more than a discount of the same amount. In negotiation, each side tends to code its own concessions as losses and the other side's concessions as gains, so that both feel they are giving more than they receive, which makes agreement harder than a simple reckoning of interests would suggest. In law, the fourfold pattern predicts that plaintiffs with strong cases will settle too cheaply, that defendants with weak cases will fight on too long, and that claims with little chance of success have a nuisance value above their expected worth. In marketing, free trials and money back guarantees work by moving the reference point, so that returning the product is experienced as giving up something already owned.
For all its success, prospect theory has serious limitations, and an honest account must set them out. The most fundamental concerns the reference point. The theory says that outcomes are judged relative to a reference point, but it does not say what the reference point is. In the simple gambles of the laboratory the status quo is the natural candidate, but in real life there are many candidates: what one has, what one expected, what one paid, what one hoped for, what one's neighbour has. A theory with a free parameter of this kind can be made to fit almost any observation after the fact, and critics have fairly complained that some applications choose the reference point that makes the story work. The most systematic response has come from Botond Kőszegi and Matthew Rabin, who proposed that the reference point is a person's rational expectation of the outcome. On this view a wage of a given size is felt as a loss if a higher one was expected and as a gain if a lower one was expected. The proposal gives the theory discipline and has had some empirical success, but the evidence is mixed, and the question of how reference points are formed and how quickly they move remains open.
A second line of criticism targets loss aversion itself. Some researchers have argued that the endowment effect in particular owes much to experimental procedure. Charles Plott and Kathryn Zeiler found that the gap between buying and selling prices shrank or vanished when subjects were given training, anonymity and incentives designed to remove misunderstandings, though others reply that these procedures remove the feeling of ownership along with the confusion. John List showed that experienced dealers at sports card conventions trade away their endowments readily, which suggests that the effect weakens with market experience and may matter less among professionals than among novices. David Gal and Derek Rucker have gone further and questioned whether losses loom larger than gains as a general principle, pointing out that many findings attributed to loss aversion can also be explained by inertia or by the allocation of attention, and that the asymmetry is weak or absent for small stakes. The balance of the evidence still favours loss aversion as a real and widespread phenomenon, especially where the stakes are meaningful, but it is better understood as a tendency that varies with context than as a fixed ratio.
A third criticism concerns the way risks are learned. Almost all the original evidence came from decisions from description, in which outcomes and probabilities are stated outright. Ralph Hertwig and colleagues showed that when people learn about a gamble by sampling its outcomes, as they do in most of life, they behave as though rare events carry too little weight rather than too much, in part because small samples often contain no instance of the rare event at all. This gap between description and experience limits the reach of the weighting function. A fourth criticism is that prospect theory, like the theory it replaced, is an account of what people choose and not of how they choose. Gerd Gigerenzer and his collaborators argue that people do not weigh and add at all, but use simple rules that inspect the features of a gamble in sequence and stop at the first one that settles the matter, and they have shown that such rules can reproduce many of the classic findings. Other rivals take different routes. Regret theory, developed by Graham Loomes and Robert Sugden, holds that people anticipate the pain of having chosen wrongly and act to avoid it. Michael Birnbaum has devised choice problems on which cumulative prospect theory makes clear predictions that his subjects systematically contradict. No single alternative has displaced prospect theory, but together they show that it is a good approximation rather than the final truth.
There are also things the theory leaves out. Kahneman himself drew attention to its inability to handle disappointment and regret. In prospect theory, winning nothing is assigned a value of zero whatever the circumstances, yet failing to win when the chance of a large prize was 90 percent is a painful experience, while failing to win when the chance was one in a million is no experience at all. The value of an outcome depends on what else might have happened, and the theory has no means of expressing this. Economists have found it difficult to apply for a related reason. Most economic situations are not single choices between well defined gambles, and to use the theory one must decide what counts as a gain or a loss, over what period outcomes are evaluated and how separate risks are grouped. Barberis, reviewing three decades of applications, concluded that these choices of modelling are the main reason why a theory so widely admired took so long to enter the mainstream of economic analysis.
Against these difficulties must be set a record of robustness that is unusual in the behavioural sciences. During the years in which many celebrated findings in psychology failed to replicate, the core results of prospect theory held. In 2020 a team led by Kai Ruggeri repeated the problems of the 1979 paper with more than four thousand participants in nineteen countries and thirteen languages, and found that the original patterns reappeared in the great majority of cases, though with somewhat smaller effects. Studies using brain imaging have found that regions involved in valuation respond more strongly to prospective losses than to equivalent gains, and that individual differences in this neural asymmetry predict individual differences in choice. Loss aversion and reference dependence have even been observed in capuchin monkeys trained to trade tokens for food, which suggests that the tendencies the theory describes are older than money and older than our species.
The wider influence of the theory is hard to overstate. It gave the emerging field of behavioural economics its first rigorous model, and it showed economists that departures from rationality need not be random noise that cancels out in the aggregate, but can be systematic, predictable and expressed in mathematics. Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2002 for this work. Tversky had died in 1996 and so could not share an award that Kahneman always insisted belonged to them both. Thaler, who did more than anyone to carry their ideas into economics, received the same prize in 2017. The concepts have spread into medicine, law, political science, public policy and management, and terms such as loss aversion and framing have passed into everyday speech.
Prospect theory is best understood as a description of how the human mind assigns value under uncertainty, offered without any claim that the mind does so wisely. It keeps the basic architecture of the theory it criticised, with values multiplied by weights and summed, and changes only what observation demanded: the carriers of value, the asymmetry between gains and losses, and the treatment of probability. That restraint is a large part of why it succeeded. It was close enough to the standard model for economists to use it and different enough to explain what the standard model could not. It does not tell us how we should choose. Expected utility theory remains a reasonable guide to that question, and one practical use of prospect theory is to warn us where our intuitions will lead us away from it: when we refuse a favourable bet because the possible loss stings, when we hold a failing investment to avoid admitting defeat, when we pay heavily to remove a trivial risk, or when a change of wording changes our mind. Nearly half a century after its publication it remains the best available summary of how people weigh risk, and its central lesson, that we live by changes and not by states and that what we stand to lose weighs more with us than what we stand to gain, is as well established as anything in the study of human decision making.
