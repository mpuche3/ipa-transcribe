The Bug That Never Bit
On the last night of 1999, engineers in a dozen countries sat in offices waiting for midnight. Companies had set up command centers with phone lines and coffee. Governments had assembled task forces, and newspapers had printed advice about canned food and cash. The fear was not war, or weather, or any of the ordinary shapes that disaster takes. The fear was a date. When the clocks rolled from 1999 to 2000, an entire generation of computer systems might get the year wrong, and nobody knew what would follow.
The trouble began with a decision that had been reasonable when it was made. The first business computers had almost no memory, and every byte was precious. Storing a year as four digits rather than two wasted space that nobody could spare, so programmers saved the two digits and left the century implied. A date like March 15, 1987, went into the machine as 870315, and everyone understood that the missing nineteen was there in spirit.
The arrangement worked for as long as the century lasted. On January 1, 2000, a program that compared two-digit years would read 00 as 1900. Interest calculations spanning a century would produce nonsense. Records created in 2000 would sort before records created in 1999 and would appear at the bottom of every list. Some systems would reject the new date outright. A smaller group faced a stranger problem: because 1900 was not a leap year, software that treated 00 as 1900 would refuse to accept February 29, 2000, a date that genuinely existed.
Warnings were available long before the deadline. An American computer pioneer named Bob Bemer, who had worked on the standards that let machines exchange text, was writing about the two-digit year in the early 1970s. He kept writing about it through the following decade. Very little happened. The people who built these systems did not expect them to still be running thirty years later, and the people who ran the organizations had no reason to think about a date that had not arrived.
Awareness came properly in the 1990s, when companies and agencies began to look at what they actually owned. What they found was discouraging. The problem was not confined to a few old programs. It sat in mainframes, in spreadsheets that had grown into critical tools, in software that nobody had documentation for, and in embedded chips inside elevators, hospital equipment, and factory machinery that no inventory had ever listed.
The repair work was less exciting than the predictions and far larger. Every system needed to be found, catalogued, examined, changed, and tested. Two strategies competed. The honest one expanded the year to four digits, which was expensive and slow. The cheap one, called windowing, taught a program a rule about which century a two-digit year belonged to, which bought time and created a new deadline for somebody else. Testing was the hardest part. A repaired system needed to be proved correct for 2000 and then proved still correct for 1999, and no team could exercise every path through a program that had been growing for twenty years.
The bill was enormous. Estimates of the worldwide cost ran into the hundreds of billions of dollars, and the money bought a mixture of genuine engineering, consulting, and paperwork. Whatever else it was, the effort became one of the largest coordinated projects in the history of computing, and it had a deadline that could not be moved. There was no version of the year 2000 that could be postponed until the work was ready.
Midnight moved west across the planet, from the Pacific islands to Australia to Asia to Europe and then to the Americas. The first reports arrived, then more, and almost all of them were dull. A handful of genuine failures surfaced: a ground station that processed satellite images, some minor trouble at a nuclear facility, scattered problems at shops and banks. A few countries reported worse luck than others. Nothing arrived that resembled the predictions, and by the morning of January 1 the story had become a joke.
The silence is harder to interpret than it looks. If the repairs worked, the world would be quiet. If the danger had been exaggerated, the world would also be quiet. The result alone can never separate the two, and nobody can rerun the year 2000 without the repairs. The engineers who spent those years hunting down two-digit years were not running a controlled experiment. They were running a rescue.
The skeptical account deserves a hearing. Many date fields were never used in the comparisons that mattered, so a wrong year sat harmlessly in storage. Many systems were replaced in the ordinary course of business during the 1990s anyway. Some of the failures that would have occurred were survivable, and would have been corrected within a week. On this account, a great deal of the money was wasted, and the panic distracted attention from risks that were real.
The truth is probably mixed, and it is certainly unknowable. What can be said is that the repairs were real work, done by real people, on a schedule that could not slip. The systems that were fixed are still running quietly in banks and hospitals and power stations, and they will never be thanked, because nothing happened.
There is a lesson here about warnings in general, and it is not a comfortable one. A disaster that is prevented leaves nothing behind. The preparations look like an overreaction, the money looks wasted, and the people who warned look like alarmists. Then the next warning of the same kind arrives, and the memory of the quiet night makes it easier to do nothing.
What the episode did show is that a diffuse, unglamorous, invisible problem can be fixed at global scale when the deadline is hard and the failure is vivid. It also showed how quickly the world forgets that it was ever at risk. The bug never bit, and the reason it never bit is that an enormous number of people spent years making sure of it.
