Move 37.
On March 9, 2016, a move was played in a board game that signaled a profound shift in our understanding of artificial intelligence. During the second game of the historic match between Lee Sedol, 18-time world champion of Go, and AlphaGo, an AI system developed by DeepMind, something remarkable happened. On move 37 of the game, AlphaGo placed a stone on the fifth line from the edge of the board, a move so unexpected and counterintuitive that commentators initially thought it was a mistake. This moment, now famously known as "Move 37," has come to represent a watershed in the development of artificial intelligence.
The ancient game of Go had long been considered the final frontier for AI in board games. While chess had fallen to computers when IBM's Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in 1997, Go presented a far greater challenge due to its vast complexity. With more possible board configurations than atoms in the observable universe, Go cannot be mastered through brute-force calculation alone. The game requires a level of intuition and pattern recognition that many believed only humans could possess.
AlphaGo's approach to the game fundamentally differed from previous game-playing AI systems. Rather than relying solely on pre-programmed rules or exhaustive search algorithms, AlphaGo combined deep neural networks with reinforcement learning. It learned by analyzing millions of human games and then by playing against itself, generating new strategies and approaches beyond human convention. This combination of supervised learning and self-play allowed AlphaGo to develop its own understanding of Go's strategic principles.
Move 37 demonstrated that AlphaGo had developed something akin to creativity. The move was not simply unusual—it represented a genuine innovation in Go strategy. According to AlphaGo's own evaluation, no human professional would have considered playing this move; the probability was estimated at perhaps one in ten thousand. Yet the AI system calculated that this move had a high probability of leading to a winning position. The move was beautiful in its subtlety, influencing the entire board while appearing almost irrelevant to the immediate tactical situation.
What made Move 37 particularly significant was not merely its deviation from human convention but the reaction it provoked. Fan Hui, the European Go champion who had previously lost to AlphaGo, stated that when he saw the move, he needed to leave the room to regain his composure. Lee Sedol, visibly shaken, took nearly fifteen minutes to respond—an eternity in professional Go. The move challenged centuries of accumulated human wisdom about the game and suggested that the AI had developed strategic insights beyond human understanding.
The technological achievement behind Move 37 represents a convergence of several important developments in AI research. AlphaGo's neural networks were trained to predict human moves and evaluate board positions, while its Monte Carlo tree search algorithm allowed it to explore possible futures and select optimal moves. This combination enabled the system to balance exploitation of known strategies with exploration of new possibilities. The result was an AI that could make moves that appeared alien to human experts but proved effective in practice.
Beyond its significance for Go, Move 37 has broader implications for our understanding of artificial intelligence. It demonstrated that AI systems can develop novel solutions to complex problems, solutions that may lie outside human conceptual frameworks. This suggests that AI might be capable of genuine creativity—not merely recombining existing ideas but generating truly original insights. Such capability challenges our understanding of creativity as an exclusively human trait.
Move 37 also highlighted the potential complementarity between human and machine intelligence. Rather than simply replacing human expertise, AlphaGo expanded our collective understanding of Go. Since the match, professional players have incorporated AlphaGo's insights into their own play, exploring strategies that were previously overlooked. The AI system became not just an opponent but a teacher, revealing new dimensions of a game humans have studied for millennia.
The societal impact of Move 37 extends far beyond the world of board games. It serves as a concrete example of how AI might transform other domains of human expertise. If an AI can discover novel strategies in a domain as thoroughly explored as Go, what might similar systems achieve in scientific research, medical diagnosis, or artistic creation? Move 37 hints at a future where AI systems complement human creativity rather than merely automating routine tasks.
However, Move 37 also raises important questions about the interpretability of AI systems. While we know that AlphaGo evaluated the move as having a high probability of success, the precise reasoning behind this evaluation remains opaque. The neural networks at the heart of the system encode their knowledge in ways that are not easily translated into human-understandable explanations. This "black box" nature of advanced AI systems presents challenges for domains where understanding the rationale behind decisions is crucial.
The legacy of Move 37 continues to evolve as AI research progresses. DeepMind's subsequent system, AlphaGo Zero, learned Go entirely through self-play, without human data, and surpassed AlphaGo's strength. This suggests that Move 37 was not merely an anomaly but the first glimpse of a new approach to problem-solving—one that may eventually extend beyond games to address complex real-world challenges.
Move 37 remains a powerful symbol of both the progress and potential of artificial intelligence. It represents a moment when a machine displayed behavior that appeared, to human experts, to be genuinely creative and insightful. As AI systems continue to develop, we may look back on this seemingly simple placement of a black stone on a wooden board as the moment when artificial intelligence began to transcend, rather than merely mimic, human cognition.
