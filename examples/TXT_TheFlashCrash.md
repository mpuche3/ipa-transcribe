The Flash Crash
On the afternoon of May sixth, 2010, the American stock market did something it had never done before. In the space of about thirty-six minutes, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped nearly one thousand points, wiping out almost a trillion dollars in value, and then, almost as strangely, it climbed most of the way back before the closing bell. Traders who had watched markets for decades had no framework for what they were seeing. Nothing like it had a name yet. Reporters would soon start calling it the flash crash.
The trouble began with a single large trade. A mutual fund company used an automated program to sell a huge batch of futures contracts tied to the stock market, spreading the sale out to avoid disrupting prices too suddenly. But the program was tied only to trading volume, not to price, and when high-frequency trading firms started buying and reselling those same contracts to each other at rapid speed, volume spiked without any of it representing real demand. The selling algorithm interpreted that spike as room to sell faster, so it did, pouring more contracts into a market that could not actually absorb them.
What followed is often described as a hot potato effect. Automated trading firms began passing the same contracts back and forth between themselves in fractions of a second, each one trying to offload the risk before anyone else could. As this frantic churn accelerated, actual buyers and sellers with genuine interest in the underlying stocks pulled back entirely, unwilling to trade blind into such a chaotic market. Liquidity, the simple ability to find someone willing to buy or sell at a reasonable price, briefly vanished.
The results were bizarre. Well-known, valuable companies saw their shares trade for a single penny, while a few other stocks briefly traded for prices as absurd as one hundred thousand dollars a share. None of these prices reflected anything real; they were artifacts of a market that had, for a few minutes, stopped functioning as a market at all. Automated systems on both sides kept executing trades against numbers that made no sense, because nothing in their programming told them to pause and ask whether the numbers were sane.
Regulators spent months untangling exactly what had happened, eventually placing significant blame on that single oversized sell order and on the destabilizing feedback loop it triggered among high-speed trading programs. A trader operating from a house outside London was later accused of placing and canceling huge numbers of orders in a way designed to trick the market, a practice called spoofing, which investigators argued helped set the stage for the collapse. In response, exchanges introduced circuit breakers, automatic pauses that halt trading in a stock after a sudden violent price swing, giving human judgment a moment to catch up with machines that had learned to trade faster than any person ever could.
