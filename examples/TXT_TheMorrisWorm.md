The Worm That Broke the Internet
In the fall of 1988, the internet was still a small, trusting place, little more than a few thousand computers at universities and research labs, all connected by a network called the ARPANET. On the second of November that year, a graduate student named Robert Morris, studying at Cornell, released a small program onto that network. He later said he only wanted to measure how large the internet had quietly grown. Instead, he built one of the first real disasters in the history of computing.
The program was a worm, a piece of software built to copy itself from one machine to the next without any person telling it to. It found its way in through a handful of known weaknesses. One was a flaw in a program called fingerd, which let users look up who was logged into a remote machine. Another was a hidden shortcut left inside the mail-delivery program sendmail. A third simply guessed at passwords, trying common words and obvious variations until one worked. None of these weaknesses were secret exactly, but almost nobody expected them to be used all at once, by a single piece of code, moving on its own.
What turned a clever experiment into a catastrophe was a single design decision. To avoid easy detection, the worm was built to keep spreading even onto machines it had already infected, on the assumption that system administrators might set up fake, harmless copies of itself as a trap. So it would ask each machine whether it was already running the program, and even when the answer was yes, it would still copy itself over again some fraction of the time, just to be safe. In practice, this meant infected computers ended up running not one copy of the worm but dozens, then hundreds, all competing for the same memory and processing power, until the machines slowed to a crawl and finally stopped responding altogether.
Within about a day, the worm had spread to roughly six thousand computers, a significant share of the entire internet at the time. Universities across the country began disconnecting their networks just to make the flood of copies stop. Administrators worked through the night comparing notes over the phone, since email itself had become unreliable, trying to understand what they were looking at and how to kill it. Total losses were later estimated anywhere from a hundred thousand to ten million dollars, mostly in lost time and idle machines.
The event permanently changed how the growing network thought about itself. Within months, the government funded the first computer emergency response team, a group built specifically to coordinate reactions to incidents like this one. Morris himself was charged and became the first person convicted under a new federal computer fraud law, though he went on to a long career in computer science. What the worm left behind was a simple, uncomfortable lesson: a network built on mutual trust between machines could be unraveled by a single piece of code that was never meant to cause any harm at all.
