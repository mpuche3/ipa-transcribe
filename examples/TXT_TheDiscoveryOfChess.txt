Discovery of Chess
I remember the day that changed everything as if it were yesterday, though it's been decades now. I was six years old, living in that small apartment in Brooklyn with my mother Regina and my sister Joan. The wallpaper in our living room had a faded floral pattern, peeling slightly at the corners where the humidity had gotten to it. We didn't have much – Mom worked as a nurse, teacher, and took whatever other jobs she could find to keep us fed and clothed. She was rarely home.
It was a wet spring afternoon. Rain tapped against the windows like impatient fingers, and the radiator in the corner hissed and clanked. Joan was eleven then, always reading something or other, her dark hair falling over her eyes as she hunched over her books. I was bored – the kind of restless boredom that makes a kid open every drawer and cabinet in the house looking for something, anything interesting.
In the bottom drawer of an old cabinet, beneath some papers and forgotten mail, I found a small cardboard box. It wasn't particularly special – just worn at the edges with a faded image on top. But when I opened it, I discovered an entire world inside.
"Joan," I called out, "what's this?"
She glanced up from her book, annoyed at first, then curious. "It's a chess set. Mom got it at a thrift store, I think."
The pieces were plastic, nothing fancy. Some were chipped, and the black knights had faded to a dull gray. The board was cardboard, creased down the middle where it folded. But there was something about those pieces – the way they stood like little soldiers, the mysterious shapes of the bishops with their slashed tops, the rooks like tiny castles. I lined them up on the board, not knowing their proper places.
"Do you know how to play?" I asked Joan.
"Sort of," she said, putting her book down. "I learned a little at school."
The rain continued to drum against the windows as Joan explained the basics. Knights move in an L-shape. Bishops move diagonally. The queen is the most powerful piece. I absorbed every word, watching her hands as she demonstrated each move on the board. There was something hypnotic about it – not just a game, but a puzzle, a battle, a story unfolding with each move.
"Checkmate means the king is trapped," she explained. "That's how you win."
We played our first game right there on the worn carpet, the pieces wobbling on the uneven surface. Joan won easily, of course. She was five years older and had played before. But I wasn't discouraged. If anything, losing made me more determined.
"Again," I demanded when the game was over.
Joan smiled, maybe a little surprised by my intensity. "Okay, Bobby. One more game."
One more game turned into three, then five. By the time Mom came home, tired from her shift, her coat damp from the rain, we were still playing. I'd lost every game, but I was beginning to see patterns, beginning to understand.
"What's all this?" Mom asked, setting down her purse.
"Bobby found the chess set," Joan explained. "He's actually pretty good for a beginner."
Mom watched us for a moment, then went to make dinner. I don't think she realized that day what had happened – how could she? How could anyone have known that in that ordinary moment, in our modest Brooklyn apartment, something extraordinary had begun?
The next morning, I woke up thinking about chess. The pieces, the moves, the possibilities. While getting dressed for school, I replayed games in my head. During breakfast, I asked Joan more questions – Why can't the king move more than one square? Why is the queen so powerful? At school, I drew chess boards in my notebook instead of listening to my teacher.
After school, I raced home, impatient to get back to the chess set. Joan was becoming less interested – she had her own friends, her own hobbies. But she played with me when I begged enough, and I soaked up every bit of knowledge she could offer.
When she couldn't play, I set up the board anyway, playing both sides, trying different moves, different strategies. I began to feel a strange connection to the game, as if the sixty-four squares were a world more real to me than the streets of Brooklyn.
Within weeks, I had mastered the basic rules. I beat Joan for the first time about a month after finding the set. She was surprised, maybe a little annoyed, but I was elated. It felt like climbing a mountain, reaching a summit I'd been straining toward.
But beating Joan wasn't enough. I wanted more – more games, more challenges, more knowledge about this world I'd discovered. I started asking Mom for chess books. She was puzzled by my sudden obsession but noticed how it kept me focused, quiet, occupied.
"It's just a phase," I overheard her telling our neighbor Mrs. Feldman one evening. "All kids get fixated on something for a while."
But it wasn't a phase. The more I played, the more I wanted to play. The more I learned, the more I realized there was to learn. The chess set became my constant companion. I carried pieces in my pocket, feeling their shapes with my fingers during school. I set up chess problems at the dinner table until Mom made me clear them away.
Sometimes, I'd wake in the middle of the night with a chess position in my head, some puzzle I'd been trying to solve. I'd slide out of bed, careful not to wake Joan in the bed across the room, and set up the board on the floor, using the faint light from the street lamps outside our window to see the pieces.
There's something about chess that's hard to explain to people who don't feel it. It's not just a game. It's a language, a science, an art. Each game is a conversation, a battle of ideas. And for a kid who often felt out of place, who didn't quite fit in with the rough-and-tumble boys in the neighborhood, chess offered something precious: a place where only your mind mattered.
By the time I turned seven, I could beat not just Joan but any kid in the neighborhood who was willing to play me. Most weren't willing more than once. I started looking for new opponents, new challenges. The local park had concrete chess tables where old men gathered to play, their fingers gnarled with age, their moves deliberate and slow.
The first time I approached them, they laughed. A skinny kid with a serious face asking to play. But their laughter stopped when I started moving the pieces. I didn't win at first – these men had been playing longer than I'd been alive – but I held my own, and they noticed.
"Where'd you learn to play like that, kid?" one of them asked me after a particularly long game.
"I taught myself," I answered, which was mostly true by then.
The old man nodded, a kind of respect in his eyes that I wasn't used to seeing from adults. "You've got something special," he said. "A natural talent."
I didn't know about talent. I just knew that when I played chess, the world made sense in a way it didn't otherwise. On the chess board, there were rules that couldn't be broken, consequences that couldn't be avoided, beauty that couldn't be denied. If you made the right moves, you won. If you made mistakes, you lost. It was fair in a way life often wasn't.
My mother began to worry about the intensity of my interest. "Bobby, maybe you should go out and play baseball with the other boys," she suggested one Saturday morning as I sat hunched over my chess board.
I looked up at her, genuinely puzzled. "Why would I want to do that?"
She sighed, a sound I'd come to recognize as her being concerned but not knowing what to do about it. "It's not normal for a boy your age to spend so much time alone with a board game."
"It's not a game," I said, with all the seriousness a seven-year-old could muster. "It's chess."
Looking back now, I understand her concern. There's something unsettling about a child with a single-minded obsession. But chess wasn't something I did; it was becoming who I was. Each game, each problem solved, each strategy mastered was like adding another room to the house of my mind. I was building myself, piece by piece, move by move.
By the time I was eight, I'd outgrown the old men in the park. I needed new challenges, stronger opponents. And that's when I discovered the Brooklyn Public Library, with its chess books and chess clubs. But that's another chapter entirely.
What started with a worn cardboard box found in a drawer became the defining force of my life. Some might call it an obsession, and maybe it was. But it was also a calling, a purpose, a path that would lead me places I couldn't have imagined that rainy afternoon when I first lined up those plastic pieces on a creased board.
Chess found me, or I found chess. Either way, from that moment on, we were inseparable. I had discovered not just a game, but the thing I was born to do. There's a unique kind of clarity in that – a certainty that few people ever experience. For all the complications that would come later in my life, I remain grateful for that clarity, for that moment of discovery that set everything in motion.
