A Home or an Asset? Ideologies and the Housing Market Across the World and Through History
Oct 6, 2026 · @Miguel Puche
A house is two things at once. It is a place to live, as basic a need as food or clothing, and it is usually the most valuable asset a family will ever own. No other object in ordinary life carries both roles so heavily. Bread is a necessity but nobody saves for retirement in bread. Shares are an investment but nobody sleeps in them. A home is both, and almost every disagreement about housing policy comes from which of these two natures a person treats as primary.
Those who see the asset first tend to want a free market in which homes are bought, sold and invested in like shares or gold. Rising prices are then a sign of success, the reward for prudent saving, and the proper task of government is to protect property and stay out of the way. Those who see the need first tend to want housing shielded from that market. For them a rising price is a rising barrier, access to a decent home is a right of citizenship, and the proper task of government is to guarantee it, whether by building, regulating or owning.
Between these poles lie several distinct traditions, and they do not line up neatly from right to left. Conservatives and libertarians both defend private ownership but for different reasons and with different policies. Social democrats and socialists both distrust the market but disagree about whether to tame it or replace it. The followers of Henry George scramble the picture altogether by treating the building as fully private and the land beneath it as common wealth. Outside Europe and North America, other answers have emerged: the state as universal landlord and developer in Singapore, the constitutional "social function of property" in Brazil, and the vast informal settlements where a billion people have housed themselves with no ideology at all.
Behind the variety, three questions recur. The first is what a house is for: an investment, a foundation for family and community, or a basic need. The second concerns land, which is the part of a home that cannot be manufactured. When a city grows and the value of a plot rises through no effort of its owner, who should receive that gain: the owner, the state or the community? The third is whom to trust with allocation. One side trusts prices and fears officials. The other trusts public institutions and fears landlords.
This essay follows these questions across time and across the world. It begins with the long centuries in which land was not a commodity at all, moves through the liberal revolution that made it one, and then takes each modern tradition in turn, with the experiments it inspired and the criticisms it has attracted. It then looks at the places where the traditions meet and clash today, from rent control to tourist flats, and at Spain as a country in which nearly all of them have left a mark. The aim throughout is to explain each view as its supporters would recognise it, not to choose among them. The figures given are approximate and intended to convey scale.
Before the market: land, custom and obligation
The idea that a home and its land can be bought and sold as freely as a sack of grain is recent. For most of history, and in most places, land was bound up with kinship, religion and political authority, and the right to occupy it came with duties attached. Understanding this older world matters, because several modern ideologies are attempts either to complete the escape from it or to recover something that was lost in the escape.
Ancient Rome is the great exception that shows how old the commercial view can be. Roman law recognised a strong form of private ownership, and the city of Rome developed a rental market that a modern reader would recognise. Most of its inhabitants lived in insulae, apartment blocks of several storeys built by investors and let through layers of middlemen. Cicero drew income from such buildings and wrote to a friend, with some amusement, that two of his properties had collapsed and that even the mice had moved out. Crassus is said to have built part of his fortune by buying burning buildings at distressed prices. The emperors responded with what may be the first building regulations aimed at speculative construction, with Augustus limiting heights to about seventy Roman feet. The pattern of private profit, poor quality and reluctant regulation was already in place two thousand years ago.
Medieval Europe organised land on a different principle. Under feudal tenure nobody below the king owned land outright in the modern sense. A lord held his estate from a superior in exchange for military or other service, and peasants held their plots from the lord in exchange for labour or rent. These holdings could be inherited but not freely sold, and they came with rights over shared resources: pasture, woodland and waste land known as the commons. A cottage was not an asset to be traded. It was a position in a web of obligations.
Religious traditions reinforced the idea that land was not simply merchandise. The book of Leviticus has God declare that the land must not be sold permanently because it belongs to him, and it prescribes a Jubilee every fifty years in which rural land returns to the family that originally held it. Interestingly, the same text treats houses inside walled cities differently, allowing them to be sold for good after a year, which is an early recognition that urban property behaves more like a commodity. In the Islamic world the institution of the waqf allowed an owner to endow property permanently for a charitable purpose, removing it from the market forever. Over the centuries a substantial share of the urban property in cities such as Istanbul, Cairo and Damascus came to be held in this way, financing mosques, schools, fountains and housing for the poor.
Other civilisations placed the state at the centre. Under the equal-field system used in China between roughly the fifth and eighth centuries, the imperial government assigned farmland to households according to their size and took it back for redistribution at death. In much of sub-Saharan Africa, the Pacific and the Americas, land belonged to the lineage or the community, including its ancestors and descendants, and a chief or council allocated the right to use it. Customary tenure of this kind still governs a large part of the land in Africa today, and it sits uneasily beside the title deeds and mortgages that formal housing markets require.
Charitable housing also has deep roots. The Fuggerei in Augsburg, founded by the banker Jakob Fugger around 1520 for needy Catholic citizens of the town, is often described as the oldest social housing estate still in use. Its residents continue to pay a nominal annual rent equivalent to one Rhenish guilder, less than a single euro, together with a promise of daily prayers for the founder's family. Almshouses in England and the Netherlands worked on similar lines. Housing the poor was understood as a duty of the rich, discharged through gift, not as a right enforceable against the state.
The transition away from this world was slow and often violent. In England, landowners enclosed the open fields and commons over several centuries, first informally and then through thousands of Acts of Parliament between about 1750 and 1850, which converted millions of acres from shared use to exclusive private property. Supporters saw enclosure as the foundation of agricultural improvement. Critics, then and since, saw it as the moment when ordinary people lost their customary claim on the land and became dependent on wages and rented rooms. Similar processes followed elsewhere: the Highland Clearances in Scotland, the breaking up of communal lands in nineteenth century Mexico and Spain, and the imposition of European property law in colonies from India to Kenya.
By the time the industrial city appeared, land had become what it had rarely been before: an asset that could be owned absolutely, sold to anyone, borrowed against and held for gain. Modern housing ideologies begin from that fact. Some celebrate it as liberation, some regret it as dispossession, and most try in one way or another to manage its consequences.
The liberal revolution and the industrial city
The philosophical case for private property in land was set out most influentially by John Locke at the end of the seventeenth century. In his Second Treatise of Government he argued that the earth was given to humanity in common, but that a person who mixes his labour with a piece of it, by clearing, planting or building, makes it his own. Property on this account is a natural right that exists before government, and the main purpose of government is to protect it. Locke added a condition that later writers would return to again and again: appropriation is legitimate where there is "enough, and as good" left for others. In an empty continent that proviso is easily met. In a crowded city it is not.
The classical economists who followed Locke defended markets and property, yet they were strikingly uneasy about land. Adam Smith observed that landlords love to reap where they never sowed, and he thought ground rents an especially suitable thing to tax, since the tax could not discourage the production of land. David Ricardo showed in 1817 that rent arises from the scarcity of good locations and grows as population and wealth increase, without any contribution from the landlord. John Stuart Mill described this gain as an unearned increment and remarked that landlords grow richer in their sleep. Thomas Paine went further in Agrarian Justice in 1797, arguing that every owner of land owed the community a ground rent, out of which each citizen should receive a payment on reaching adulthood. The liberal tradition therefore contained, from the beginning, both the strongest defence of property and the seed of the most persistent objection to property in land.
The industrial revolution turned these arguments from theory into emergency. Cities such as Manchester, Glasgow, Berlin and New York grew faster than any had before, and the market housed the new workers in the cheapest way it could. Friedrich Engels described the cellar dwellings and back-to-back houses of Manchester in 1845. Berlin became known for its Mietskasernen, the "rental barracks" built around successive dark courtyards. In New York, the journalist Jacob Riis photographed the tenements of the Lower East Side for his 1890 book How the Other Half Lives, showing families of ten in single unventilated rooms. Cholera and tuberculosis did not respect class boundaries, and it was fear of epidemic as much as compassion that first moved governments to act.
The earliest responses stayed within liberal principles. The state would not house people, but it would set minimum standards for what the market could build. Britain's Public Health Act of 1875 allowed towns to impose by-laws on street width, drainage and ventilation, producing the regular terraces still visible in English cities. New York's Tenement House Act of 1901 required windows, toilets and fire escapes. Regulation of this kind improved quality but raised costs, and the poorest were often displaced instead of rehoused. When Baron Haussmann rebuilt central Paris for Napoleon III between 1853 and 1870, the grand boulevards replaced crowded working class districts, and their inhabitants were pushed to the periphery, an early case of what would later be called gentrification by public works.
Philanthropy offered a second response. In London the American banker George Peabody endowed a trust in 1862 to build decent flats for the working poor, and other investors followed with what was called five per cent philanthropy, the idea that model dwellings could pay a modest return while doing good. The reformer Octavia Hill bought run-down houses with money lent by John Ruskin and managed them through personal visits to tenants, pioneering what became the profession of housing management. Enlightened industrialists built whole settlements for their workers: Saltaire and Port Sunlight in England, the Krupp colonies in Essen, Pullman near Chicago. These experiments proved that good housing for workers was possible. They also showed its limits, since tenants who lost their job or displeased their employer lost their home as well, a lesson made vivid by the Pullman strike of 1894.
The decisive break came with the First World War. In 1915, with men at the front and munitions workers crowding into Glasgow, landlords raised rents sharply. Thousands of women led by Mary Barbour organised a rent strike, physically blocking the officers sent to evict them. Faced with unrest in a city vital to the war effort, the British government passed an act within weeks freezing rents at their pre-war level. Similar controls appeared in France, Germany, Austria and New York. Measures introduced as temporary proved very hard to remove, and in many countries they lasted for decades.
The war left two legacies. It established that governments could and would override the market in housing when social peace required it. And by making private renting unprofitable, rent control weakened the sector that had housed most urban Europeans, which meant that someone else would have to build. The ideologies of the twentieth century can be read as competing answers to the question of who that should be: a freed market, a nation of small owners, the state, the cooperative movement, or the community as landlord of the ground itself.
Housing as a commodity: the market liberal and libertarian view
For market liberals and libertarians, a house is a good like any other, and the owner's right to buy, sell, rent or leave it empty is part of the basic right to property. The moral foundation is Lockean: what a person has built or paid for is theirs, and nobody else has a claim on it. The economic foundation is the price mechanism. When homes become expensive in a city, the market is announcing that more should be built there. Developers, landlords and investors who respond to that signal are performing a useful service, and the profit they earn is the reward for providing something people want.
On this view, a housing crisis is almost always a supply crisis created by government. The classic statement is a pamphlet written in 1946 by Milton Friedman and George Stigler called Roofs or Ceilings?, which argued that rent control in American cities was causing the very shortage it was meant to relieve. A controlled rent, they said, encourages existing tenants to occupy more space than they need, discourages owners from letting or maintaining property, and replaces rationing by price with rationing by luck and favouritism. Friedrich Hayek, who had watched rent control hollow out the private housing stock of Vienna after the First World War, made the same case. The Swedish economist Assar Lindbeck later put it most memorably when he suggested that, short of bombing, rent control was the most effective technique yet discovered for destroying a city.
Since the 1970s the argument has shifted from rents to land use. Economists such as Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko have argued that in successful cities the gap between what a home costs to build and what it sells for is explained mainly by rules that restrict building: zoning that reserves most land for detached houses, height limits, minimum plot sizes, parking requirements, protected views and lengthy approval procedures. American zoning dates from the early twentieth century, with New York adopting the first comprehensive code in 1916, and critics note that it was used from the beginning to keep poorer and non-white households out of prosperous districts. In Britain the equivalent restriction is the discretionary planning system created in 1947 and the green belts drawn around major cities.
Supporters of deregulation point to places that build freely. Japan sets zoning rules nationally, with a small number of permissive categories and little scope for neighbours to object, and Tokyo has in many years started more homes than the whole of England. Despite being one of the largest and richest cities in the world, its housing costs have stayed relatively stable for a generation. Houston has no conventional zoning code at all, and when it cut its minimum plot size in the late 1990s, tens of thousands of townhouses followed. Auckland rezoned roughly three quarters of its residential land in 2016 to allow more homes per plot, after which construction rose strongly and rents grew more slowly than in the rest of New Zealand. Minneapolis and the state of Oregon abolished zoning reserved exclusively for single family houses soon afterwards, and Austin saw rents fall after a building boom in the early 2020s.
A more radical demonstration came from Argentina. At the end of 2023 the newly elected libertarian president Javier Milei repealed a national law that had fixed minimum lease terms and limited rent increases at a time of very high inflation. Under that law many owners had withdrawn their flats from the market. After its repeal the number of flats advertised for rent in Buenos Aires was reported to have risen sharply and rents to have fallen in real terms. Supporters treat the episode as a natural experiment confirming Friedman and Stigler. Sceptics reply that tenants lost security and that the results depend heavily on Argentina's unusual economic conditions.
Market liberals do not necessarily oppose all help for the poor, but they prefer to give money to people instead of controlling prices or building public estates. Housing vouchers, such as those created in the United States in 1974, let low income tenants choose a home in the private market with the state paying part of the rent. Chile under the Pinochet government in the 1970s and 1980s built its housing policy around a one-off subsidy to buyers, combined with their own savings and a mortgage, leaving construction to private firms. The model was widely copied in Latin America. It delivered a great many homes and sharply reduced the formal housing deficit, though mostly on cheap land at the edge of cities, far from jobs.
Critics answer that housing is not an ordinary commodity and that its peculiarities are large enough to break the simple model. Land in a given location cannot be manufactured, so new supply elsewhere is an imperfect substitute. Nobody can opt out of needing shelter, which leaves tenants in a weak bargaining position. Construction takes years and buildings last a century, so supply responds slowly and markets overshoot. Where homes are also investments, rising prices can attract more buyers instead of fewer, the opposite of how demand is supposed to behave. And a purely market system has never, in any rich country, housed the poorest tenth of the population at a standard the rest of society finds acceptable.
There is also an awkward political fact for this tradition. The restrictions it blames are frequently demanded not by socialists but by homeowners, who use zoning and planning to protect the value of their own property. The freedom of one owner to build clashes with the wish of the neighbouring owner that nothing should change. Libertarians resolve this in favour of the right to build. Many of their natural allies on the right do not.
Housing as a financial product
Treating a house as a commodity is one step. Treating it as a financial instrument, comparable to shares, bonds or gold, is a further one, and it depends on a piece of machinery that took two centuries to build: the mortgage market. Few people can buy a home from savings, so the price of housing is in practice the price that lenders are willing to finance. Whoever shapes the flow of credit shapes the housing market.
The machinery began modestly. The first building societies appeared in Birmingham in the 1770s as clubs in which members pooled savings until every one of them had a house, after which the society dissolved. Permanent societies followed in Britain, and savings and loan associations in the United States. Until the 1930s, however, mortgages were short, typically five to ten years, and required large deposits. The modern long mortgage was largely a creation of government. During the Depression the Roosevelt administration set up the Federal Housing Administration to insure loans of twenty and later thirty years with small deposits, and founded Fannie Mae in 1938 to buy those loans from banks so that they could lend again. An instrument designed to rescue homeowners became the foundation of mass ownership.
From the 1970s finance took a new turn. Mortgages began to be bundled into securities and sold to investors around the world, so that a pension fund in Norway could hold a share of the debts of homeowners in Nevada. Governments in Britain, the United States, Scandinavia and elsewhere deregulated lending in the 1980s, removing limits on how much banks could lend and to whom. Credit became abundant, deposits shrank, and prices rose to absorb whatever buyers could borrow. A home increasingly came to be seen as a ladder: a first purchase would rise in value, providing the deposit for the next, and eventually a pension.
The risks of this view have been demonstrated several times. In Japan in the late 1980s land prices rose so far that the grounds of the Imperial Palace in Tokyo were said to be worth more than all the real estate in California. Prices collapsed after 1991 and urban land values fell for well over a decade, leaving banks crippled and a generation wary of property. In the United States, loans to borrowers with poor credit were packaged into securities rated as safe, and when house prices fell from 2006 the losses spread through the global financial system. National prices dropped by around a third, millions of families lost their homes to foreclosure, and the crisis of 2008 followed. Ireland and Spain had their own bubbles, financed by cheap credit after joining the euro. At its peak Spain was starting more homes each year than Germany, France and Italy combined, and Ireland was left with unfinished ghost estates across the countryside when prices fell by about half.
The aftermath deepened financial involvement instead of reducing it. Central banks cut interest rates to near zero for a decade, which pushed investors towards property in search of returns. Private equity firms bought repossessed homes in bulk. Blackstone became one of the largest owners of single family rental houses in the United States and the largest private landlord in Spain. In Germany, cities had sold their municipal housing to investors in the early 2000s, with Dresden disposing of its entire stock in one transaction in 2006, and listed companies such as Vonovia came to own hundreds of thousands of flats. In 2017 the United Nations rapporteur on housing, Leilani Farha, described this financialisation as a transformation of housing from a social good into a vehicle for wealth and investment.
Global capital added another layer. Wealthy buyers from Russia, the Gulf and China treated flats in London, Vancouver, Sydney and Miami as a safe store of value, sometimes leaving them empty. Several countries sold residence rights in exchange for property investment through so-called golden visa programmes, including Portugal from 2012 and Spain from 2013 until both later withdrew the property route. The reaction has been a wave of restrictions that would have seemed illiberal a generation ago. Switzerland has limited purchases by foreigners since the 1980s. Vancouver introduced a tax on foreign buyers in 2016 and on empty homes in 2017. New Zealand banned most purchases by non-residents in 2018 and Canada followed in 2023.
China offers the largest example of all. Urban housing there was a welfare benefit allocated by employers until 1998, when the government ended the system and created a private market almost overnight. With few other outlets for savings, households poured their wealth into flats, and property with its related industries grew to something like a quarter of the economy. In 2016 President Xi Jinping declared that houses are for living in, not for speculation, a phrase repeated in official documents for years. When the state tightened credit to developers in 2020, the giant firm Evergrande defaulted, and a long slump began that has weighed on the entire Chinese economy. It is a remarkable case of a nominally communist government confronting the consequences of housing as an investment product.
Defenders of housing finance make a serious case. Long mortgages let ordinary people acquire an asset that would otherwise belong only to the rich, and an owner's equity has been the main form of wealth for the middle classes of the twentieth century. They argue that the failures were failures of regulation or of supply, not of the idea itself, and they point to Germany, where conservative lending, a large rental sector and steady construction kept real house prices almost flat for several decades. Critics reply that once a society's savings, pensions and banks all depend on house prices never falling, governments lose the freedom to make housing cheaper. A home cannot be both a good investment and permanently affordable, since the first requires its price to rise faster than incomes and the second requires that it should not.
The property-owning democracy: the conservative view
Conservatives share the liberal attachment to private ownership but value it for social and moral reasons more than economic ones. Their argument is that people who own their homes have a stake in their neighbourhood and in the stability of the country. They look after what is theirs, they save, they are harder to radicalise, and they have something to pass on to their children. The home is the seat of the family, and the family is the basic unit of society. A market liberal is indifferent between owning and renting so long as the choice is free. A conservative is not: ownership is good in itself, and the state may properly tilt the scales towards it.
The phrase that sums up the ideal was coined by the Scottish Conservative Noel Skelton in 1923. Writing just after the vote had been extended to almost all adults, Skelton worried that political democracy without economic security would drift towards socialism, and he proposed a "property-owning democracy" in which the mass of voters would have something to conserve. Anthony Eden took up the slogan after 1945. Harold Macmillan, as housing minister in the early 1950s, won his reputation by reaching a target of three hundred thousand new homes a year, many of them still council houses but with private building steadily encouraged.
Margaret Thatcher gave the idea its most famous expression. The Housing Act of 1980 gave council tenants in Britain the Right to Buy their homes at discounts that eventually reached well over half the market value. Around two million households did so over the following decades, and home ownership rose from a little over half of households to more than two thirds. For the families concerned it was often transformative, giving them an asset, the freedom to alter their home and a sense of independence from the local authority. It was also shrewd politics, since it created a large group of new owners with reason to be grateful.
The American version is older and runs through the whole of national history. Thomas Jefferson imagined a republic of independent farmers, and the Homestead Act of 1862 offered settlers a plot of public land in return for living on it and improving it. In the 1920s Herbert Hoover promoted a national campaign to encourage families to own their own home. The New Deal mortgage institutions and the benefits granted to returning soldiers after 1945 then made it possible on a mass scale. William Levitt applied assembly line methods to build Levittown on Long Island from 1947, selling small houses to veterans for less than the cost of renting, and he remarked that no man who owns his own house and lot can be a communist because he has too much to do. Ownership in the United States rose from well under half of households in 1940 to over three fifths by 1960.
That achievement had a shadow which has become central to American debate. Federal mortgage insurance was guided by maps that marked black and mixed neighbourhoods as hazardous, a practice later known as redlining, and many of the new suburbs, Levittown included, excluded non-white buyers by contract. Since housing equity became the main source of family wealth, the families shut out of ownership in those decades were also shut out of the wealth that followed, which helps explain the large racial wealth gap that persists today. The episode is a reminder that a policy of spreading property always involves a decision about who is included.
Continental Europe had its own variant, shaped by Catholic social teaching. In the encyclical Rerum Novarum of 1891, Pope Leo XIII defended private property against socialism while insisting that the law should help as many people as possible to become owners. Christian democratic parties after 1945 took this as a programme. Belgium subsidised families to build their own houses. Italy's Christian Democrats launched a national building plan financed by a payroll levy. West Germany under Konrad Adenauer encouraged saving for a family home through dedicated savings banks, although it retained a large rental sector. In Britain the writers G. K. Chesterton and Hilaire Belloc argued under the name of distributism for a society of small proprietors as an alternative to both big capital and the big state.
Authoritarian conservatism reached the same conclusion more bluntly. In Spain, Franco's first housing minister, José Luis Arrese, declared in the late 1950s that the regime wanted a country of owners and not of proletarians. The state subsidised the construction of millions of flats for sale, and renting, which had been the norm in Spanish cities, became the exception. In Australia the conservative leader Robert Menzies built his postwar appeal on what he called the forgotten people, the middle class whose patriotism he located in the homes they owned. In each case ownership was understood as a defence against social conflict.
The usual instruments of this tradition are subsidies to buyers: tax relief on mortgage interest, grants to first time purchasers, discounted sales of public housing, guarantees for low deposit loans and favourable tax treatment of the family home. George W. Bush's "ownership society" in the early 2000s and the British Help to Buy scheme of 2013 are recent examples.
The weakness critics identify is that these policies help one generation at the expense of the next. Subsidising demand without increasing supply tends to raise prices, so the benefit passes to whoever is selling. Once a majority of voters are owners, they acquire a strong interest in rising values and in blocking construction nearby, a pattern the economist William Fischel called the homevoter effect. The council houses sold under Right to Buy were mostly not replaced, and a large share of those in London have ended up in the hands of private landlords, sometimes let back to councils at market rents to house homeless families. In Britain, the United States and Australia the rate of ownership among young adults has fallen well below that of their parents at the same age. The property-owning democracy, critics say, has become a democracy of those who already own property.
A regulated mixed system: the social democratic view
Social democrats accept a private housing market but do not trust it to house everyone decently. Housing is treated as a social right, similar to healthcare or education, which the state must guarantee where the market fails. The goal is not to abolish private ownership but to place a large, good quality alternative beside it and to regulate the relationship between landlord and tenant. The characteristic institutions are public or non-profit rental housing, security of tenure, some control over rents and allowances that help poorer households pay.
The founding experiment is known as Red Vienna. After the collapse of the Habsburg empire the city was governed from 1919 by the Social Democrats, who inherited overcrowded tenements and a private rental sector paralysed by wartime rent control. Under the finance councillor Hugo Breitner the city introduced a steeply progressive tax on housing and on luxuries, and used the proceeds to build more than sixty thousand flats by 1934. The new estates, of which the kilometre long Karl-Marx-Hof is the best known, included laundries, kindergartens, clinics and libraries, and rents were set at a small fraction of a worker's wage. The experiment ended when the Austrian right took power by force in 1934, shelling the Karl-Marx-Hof in the process, but the buildings survived and so did the policy. Today the city owns roughly 220,000 flats and supports a similar number through limited-profit associations, so that around half of Viennese live in housing outside the ordinary market.
What distinguishes Vienna is that this housing is not reserved for the poor. Income limits are high enough to admit most of the population, and tenants may stay if their income later rises. The estates are therefore socially mixed, they carry little stigma, and their rents act as an anchor for the private sector, which must compete with them. The sociologist Jim Kemeny described this as a unitary rental system, in which non-profit and private landlords compete in a single market, and contrasted it with the dualist systems of the English speaking countries, where social housing is a residual safety net kept apart from a lightly regulated private sector.
Sweden pursued a similar vision under the slogan of the people's home. Municipal housing companies built for all, not only for those in need, and between 1965 and 1974 the Million Programme added about a million dwellings in a country of eight million people. Rents were negotiated collectively between landlords and a national tenants' union, and the private sector was bound to the same levels. The Netherlands took a different institutional route to a comparable result: its Housing Act of 1901 enabled independent housing associations, which today own close to a third of all homes in the country and a larger share in Amsterdam. Denmark's non-profit sector, governed in part by its own tenants, houses about a fifth of the population.
Britain's Labour governments built on a comparable scale but with a different outcome. The first large programme followed the First World War under the promise of homes fit for heroes. After 1945 the health and housing minister Aneurin Bevan insisted on generous space standards and spoke of estates where the doctor, the grocer and the labourer would live on the same street. By the end of the 1970s about a third of British households rented from a local council. Later decades of tighter budgets, system-built tower blocks and allocation by need alone narrowed the sector towards the poorest, and the Right to Buy then removed much of the best stock. The British story is often cited as a warning of how a universal model can decline into a residual one.
France and Germany found intermediate forms. France houses roughly one household in six in its moderate-rent sector, and a law passed in 2000 obliges urban municipalities to reach a minimum share of social housing, originally a fifth and later a quarter, or pay a penalty, a measure aimed at wealthy suburbs that had refused to build any. West Germany subsidised private and non-profit builders in exchange for regulated rents over a fixed period, after which the homes returned to the market. That design built quickly but meant the social stock shrank as obligations expired, from several million homes around 1990 to little more than a million today. German tenants are nonetheless well protected, with indefinite contracts and legal limits on increases, and more than half of households rent without the arrangement being regarded as a sign of failure.
The United States shows the model in its weakest form. Public housing was authorised in 1937 but confined to the poor, at the insistence of the property industry, which feared competition. Concentrated poverty, poor maintenance and racial segregation followed, and the demolition of the Pruitt-Igoe estate in St Louis in the 1970s, less than twenty years after it opened, became a worldwide symbol of the supposed failure of public housing. Social democrats draw the opposite lesson: that housing built only for the poor will be poorly defended and poorly funded.
Finland provides a recent success of a narrower kind. Since 2008 it has followed a Housing First policy, giving homeless people a permanent flat with support, without first requiring them to resolve addiction or unemployment. Long term homelessness has fallen substantially, in contrast to almost every other European country.
Opponents point to the costs. Rents held below the market level produce queues, and in Stockholm the wait for a regulated flat commonly runs to many years, which has fostered a black market in contracts and makes it hard for newcomers to move to the city for work. Those inside the system are protected while outsiders, especially the young and immigrants, are left to an expensive unregulated fringe. Tenants with cheap contracts have little reason to move even when their home no longer suits them. And public building depends on sustained spending that is vulnerable to every fiscal crisis. Sweden itself withdrew most housing subsidies in the 1990s, and construction fell steeply. Social democrats reply that these are arguments for building more, not for regulating less.
Housing outside the market: the socialist view
Socialists go a step further than social democrats and argue that the problem is the market itself. As long as housing can be owned for profit, they say, landlords and investors will extract income from people who have no choice but to pay, and shortage will be profitable for those who own. The aim is therefore decommodification: homes should be allocated according to need, not according to who can pay most, and nobody should grow rich from another person's need for shelter.
The classic text is The Housing Question, a series of articles written by Friedrich Engels in 1872. Engels was replying to followers of the French thinker Proudhon, who proposed to solve the problem by converting rent into instalments of purchase so that every tenant would become an owner. Engels dismissed this as a petty bourgeois dream that would tie workers to one place and to their debts. He argued that the housing shortage was not an accident but a necessary product of capitalism, that reforms merely moved the slums from one district to another, and that only the abolition of the capitalist system would end it. In the meantime he noted that there were already enough buildings in the big cities to house everyone, if they were used rationally, a remark that later inspired policies of requisition.
The Soviet Union put the theory into practice. A decree of 1918 abolished private ownership of real estate in the cities, and large bourgeois flats were divided among several families, each occupying a room and sharing the kitchen and bathroom. These communal apartments, intended as temporary, remained the normal form of urban life for forty years while investment went to heavy industry. Space was allotted by a norm of a few square metres per person. Only under Khrushchev, from the late 1950s, did the state begin mass construction of small prefabricated flats in five storey blocks, moving tens of millions of people into a home of their own within a decade. Rents and utilities absorbed only a few per cent of income, and eviction was almost unknown.
The same model spread with variations across the communist world. East Germany announced a programme in the 1970s to solve the housing question as a social problem by 1990 and built vast estates of concrete panel blocks, while the old inner city tenements, whose controlled rents did not cover repairs, were left to decay. In China, urban housing was provided by the work unit, the factory or office that also supplied healthcare and schooling, and by the late 1970s the average city dweller had less living space than in the early 1950s. Yugoslavia allocated socially owned flats through self-managed enterprises, though many families simply built their own houses. Cuba followed a distinctive path: the Urban Reform Law of 1960 abolished private renting and turned tenants into owners, with their former rent counted as payment, but forbade them to sell. For half a century Cubans could only swap homes, until buying and selling were legalised in 2011.
The record of state socialism in housing is mixed in a way that neither side of the debate finds entirely comfortable. It eliminated homelessness in the ordinary sense, kept costs trivial and gave tenants near absolute security. It also produced chronic shortages, waits of ten years or more, young couples living with parents long after marriage, uniform and often shoddy construction, and neglect of anything already built, as the crumbling centre of Havana still shows. With no prices, flats were allocated through workplaces and officials, which rewarded connections and loyalty. An informal market in swaps and favours grew up to do what the formal market was forbidden to do.
The end of communism brought an ironic outcome. Most governments in Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union transferred state flats to their occupants for little or nothing, and China sold work unit housing to employees at low prices. The countries that had gone furthest in abolishing private property thus became, almost overnight, the nations with the highest rates of home ownership in the world. In Romania well over nine households in ten own their home, and the figures for Hungary, Lithuania and China are not far behind. Those societies now have very small rental sectors and little social housing, and their young people face markets as difficult as any in the West.
Contemporary socialists have largely abandoned the centralised model in favour of collective ownership on a smaller scale. Housing cooperatives are the main example. In Zurich about a fifth of all flats belong to non-profit cooperatives that charge rents based on cost, and voters decided in 2011 that the share should rise to a third. In Uruguay a law of 1968 enabled mutual aid cooperatives in which members contribute labour to build their homes and then own them collectively, so that no individual can sell at a profit. Denmark, Germany and Canada have long traditions of the same kind.
The community land trust applies the idea to the ground itself. The first was founded in 1969 in rural Georgia by veterans of the American civil rights movement, who wanted black farmers to have secure land that could not be lost to debt or pressure. A trust owns the land permanently and leases plots to residents, who own their houses but agree to resell at a limited price, so that the home stays affordable for the next occupant. The largest urban example grew out of a trust set up in Burlington, Vermont, in 1984, when Bernie Sanders was mayor, and the model has since spread to Britain, Belgium and beyond.
A more confrontational strand seeks to take existing housing out of private hands. Squatters' movements in Amsterdam, Copenhagen, Berlin and Barcelona have occupied empty buildings since the 1970s, sometimes winning legal status. In Berlin, where the city had sold tens of thousands of public flats in the early 2000s, a campaign forced a referendum in 2021 calling for the expropriation of every corporate landlord with more than three thousand units, with compensation below market value. Close to six voters in ten supported it. The city government has not carried it out, and a rent freeze passed the previous year was struck down by the constitutional court, but the vote showed how far opinion had moved.
The standard objections are the ones the historical record suggests. Removing prices removes the information and incentive that get homes built, maintained and matched to the people who want them. Allocation by committee can become allocation by connections. Expropriation spends public money on buildings that already exist instead of adding new ones, and it frightens away the private investment on which most cities still depend. Cooperatives and land trusts escape some of these criticisms, but they have remained small almost everywhere, which leaves open the question of whether they can house a whole society or only a fortunate minority within it.
Private buildings, common land: the Georgist view
Georgism does not fit on the line between left and right, because it splits the house from the ground beneath it. Its founder, the American journalist Henry George, published Progress and Poverty in 1879 after watching land speculation in California, where fortunes were made by people who had done nothing except hold title to plots near a growing city or a planned railway. He asked why poverty deepened as societies grew richer, and his answer was that the gains of progress were being captured by the owners of land in the form of rising rents.
George's argument rests on a distinction. A building is the product of someone's labour and capital, and it should belong to that person without restriction or tax. The land is different. Nobody made it, its supply is fixed, and its value comes almost entirely from what surrounds it: the transport links, schools, jobs, shops and neighbours that the community as a whole has created. A plot in the centre of a great city is worth a fortune and an identical plot in a desert is worth nothing, and the owner is responsible for none of the difference. George concluded that the rental value of land belongs by right to the community and should be collected through a tax, which he believed could replace all other taxes.
The idea had respectable ancestors. The French Physiocrats of the eighteenth century had proposed a single tax on land. Smith, Ricardo and Mill had all noted that ground rent was unearned, as described earlier. What George added was moral urgency and a gift for popular writing. His book sold in the millions and was for a time among the most widely read works in the English language. He came second in the election for mayor of New York in 1886, ahead of the young Theodore Roosevelt. Leo Tolstoy promoted his ideas in Russia, and a board game invented in 1904 by one of his followers, Lizzie Magie, to teach the evils of land monopoly was later adapted into Monopoly, with its lesson reversed.
The practical proposal is the land value tax. The owner keeps the building and everything earned from improving it, but pays an annual charge based on the value of the site alone. Several consequences follow. Because the tax falls equally on a vacant plot and a developed one, holding land idle while waiting for its price to rise becomes expensive, so speculation loses its appeal and owners are pushed to build or sell. Because the tax is not reduced by neglecting a building or increased by improving it, it does not discourage construction in the way an ordinary property tax does. And because the supply of land cannot shrink in response, economists regard it as unusually efficient. Milton Friedman called it the least bad tax, and it has been recommended over the years by figures as different as Joseph Stiglitz and the authors of official tax reviews in Britain and Australia.
Politics came close to adopting it on several occasions. In Britain the Liberal government's People's Budget of 1909, championed by David Lloyd George and Winston Churchill, proposed taxes on land values and on the increase in those values. The landowners in the House of Lords rejected the budget, provoking a constitutional crisis that ended with the Lords losing their veto, though the land taxes themselves were later abandoned. Labour governments tried three times after 1945 to capture the rise in value that follows permission to build, beginning with a full charge on development gains in 1947, and each scheme was repealed by the next Conservative government. Denmark has taxed land values since the 1920s. Australia and New Zealand introduced land taxes at the turn of the twentieth century, and many Australian councils still levy their rates on the unimproved value of the site. Pittsburgh and other cities in Pennsylvania taxed land more heavily than buildings for much of the last century. Estonia adopted a pure land tax when it regained independence.
The most intriguing chapter took place in China. In 1898 Germany leased the port of Qingdao, and its administrator, an admirer of the land reformers, imposed an annual tax on land values and a further levy on increases in value at sale, in order to prevent the speculation seen in other colonial ports. Sun Yat-sen, the founder of the Chinese republic, studied the experiment and made the equalisation of land rights one of his core principles. His followers carried it to Taiwan, where land value taxes and a tax on land value increments remain written into the constitution.
A related approach is for the public to keep ownership of the land and lease it. Ebenezer Howard's garden city movement built Letchworth, north of London, from 1903 on land held by a trust so that rising ground rents would fund the community's services. Canberra was founded on leasehold for the same reason. In Hong Kong virtually all land belongs to the government, which sells long leases at auction, and Singapore's state acquired most of the island's land after independence under a law that allowed compulsory purchase at below market prices. Both cities have financed infrastructure and kept other taxes low from land revenue.
Yet the Georgist remedy is far more often praised than adopted, and the reasons are instructive. The first is political arithmetic. George wrote when most voters were tenants, whereas in most rich countries today most voters are homeowners who would pay the tax. The second is hardship in individual cases, typified by the elderly widow in a valuable house with a small pension. The third is the technical difficulty of valuing land separately from buildings, although assessors in Denmark and Australia have done so for a century. California's voters chose the opposite principle in 1978 with Proposition 13, which caps property tax and limits reassessment until a home is sold, rewarding those who hold land longest.
Critics add points of substance. A land tax introduced today would fall on current owners who paid the full price for their land, including the capitalised value of future rents, and who would see that value fall without having done anything wrong. Hong Kong suggests that public ownership of land is no guarantee of cheap housing, since a government that lives on land sales acquires an interest in high prices. And land value alone, most economists now think, would not raise enough to replace all other taxes. Georgism endures less as a programme than as a lens. It keeps asking the question that other traditions prefer to leave implicit: when a city makes a piece of ground valuable, who is entitled to the value?
The state as developer: East Asian models
The housing systems of East Asia are hard to place on a Western ideological map. Their governments have generally been pro-business and hostile to welfare spending, yet several of them have intervened in housing more deeply than any European social democracy. The common thread is that housing was treated as an instrument of nation building and economic development, not primarily as a question of rights or of markets.
Singapore is the most complete example. When the island gained self-government in 1959, much of its population lived in overcrowded shophouses and squatter settlements. The new government under Lee Kuan Yew set up the Housing and Development Board in 1960 and built at a speed that astonished observers. Its decisive choice came in 1964, when it began selling the flats to their occupants instead of renting them. From 1968 buyers were allowed to pay with their balances in the Central Provident Fund, a compulsory savings scheme into which every worker and employer contributes, so that the mortgage was in effect deducted from wages before they were received.
The result is a system that combines elements every Western ideology would recognise and none would have designed. Roughly four in five Singaporeans live in flats built by the state, which is more than in any socialist country at its height. Around nine in ten of those households own their flat, which fulfils the conservative ideal more fully than Britain or America ever managed. The state owns most of the land and sells the flats on leases of ninety-nine years, which is close to the Georgist prescription. And a regulated resale market exists in which owners can sell at a profit, which satisfies the investor. Lee was explicit about his reasoning, which was conservative in the deepest sense. He wanted every family to have a stake in the country, he wrote, because parents whose sons were conscripted to defend it should feel they were defending something of their own.
The system is also more controlling than a Western electorate would readily accept. Eligibility depends on citizenship, income and, for most new flats, on forming a family, so that young couples commonly apply for a flat when they become engaged. A household may own only one subsidised flat and must live in it for a minimum period before selling. Since 1989 each block and neighbourhood has been subject to ethnic quotas intended to prevent the formation of enclaves. In recent years a new worry has surfaced: as the oldest leases move towards expiry, flats that families regarded as a store of wealth must eventually decline in value towards nothing, and the government has had to remind citizens that the asset they bought was a long tenancy, not a freehold.
Hong Kong started from a similar emergency and reached a very different result. A fire on Christmas night in 1953 destroyed a squatter settlement at Shek Kip Mei and left some fifty thousand people homeless, prompting the colonial government to begin a public housing programme that it had long resisted. Today close to half the population lives in public rental flats or in subsidised flats for sale. The other half faces one of the least affordable private markets on earth. Because the government owns all the land and has historically released it slowly, drawing a large part of its revenue from the sales, prices are extraordinary, and at the bottom of the private market tens of thousands of people live in subdivided flats, some of them little larger than a bed. The waiting time for a public flat stretches to several years. Hong Kong shows that public ownership of land and a large public sector are not sufficient if the supply of land is held tight.
South Korea built its cities at remarkable speed under a system dominated by private ownership of apartments and by large construction firms working to state plans. At the end of the 1980s the government launched a drive to build two million homes in five years, creating entire new towns around Seoul. Korea is also home to a unique form of tenancy known as jeonse, in which the tenant pays no monthly rent but hands the landlord a very large deposit, often well over half the value of the flat, which is returned at the end of the contract. The landlord profits by investing the deposit, and the tenant in effect saves towards a purchase. The arrangement worked while interest rates and prices were high and rising. When prices fell in the early 2020s, some landlords could not return deposits, and thousands of tenants lost their savings. Apartment prices in Seoul have meanwhile become one of the most charged issues in Korean politics, with successive governments of left and right punished by voters for failing to contain them.
Japan completes the picture with a market that differs from the Western pattern in one fundamental respect. After the war the state supported building through a public mortgage bank and a housing corporation that constructed large estates for the new middle class. But Japanese houses are not expected to hold their value. A typical wooden house is treated as worthless within twenty or thirty years and is demolished and rebuilt by the next owner, so that only the land retains a price. Combined with permissive zoning and, since the 1990s, a shrinking population, this has produced abundant and affordable housing even in Tokyo. It has also left around nine million dwellings standing empty across the country, mostly in the countryside and in declining towns. In Japan a home behaves less like an investment and more like a durable consumer good, something close to a car, which is precisely what many Western reformers say they want and what few Western homeowners would accept.
Taken together, these cases undermine any simple link between ideology and outcome. The most successful mass home ownership programme in the world was carried out by a state that owns the land and builds the flats. A territory with almost complete public land ownership has the most painful private market. A country with a liberal building regime has affordable cities and millions of empty houses. What they share is a recognition that the housing system is something governments design, whether they admit it or not, and that the design determines who gains from a growing city.
Self-built cities: the Global South
The debates described so far assume that housing is produced by builders and regulated by a capable state. For a large part of humanity neither assumption holds. The United Nations estimates that more than a billion people live in informal settlements: the favelas of Brazil, the barriadas of Peru, the bustees of India, the townships and shack settlements of Africa. Their residents have built their own homes, usually on land they do not legally own, without permits, mortgages or architects. In many cities of the developing world this is not a marginal phenomenon but the main way housing gets produced. The ideological contest has therefore centred on a different question: what should be done about housing that already exists outside the law?
The first official answer, through the 1950s and 1960s, was to demolish it and build public estates on the European model. This failed almost everywhere. Governments could not afford to build at the necessary scale, the flats that were built went to civil servants and the better connected, and the people evicted from the slums simply built new ones further out.
A different view was put forward by the English architect John Turner, who worked in the squatter settlements of Lima from the late 1950s. Turner, whose outlook was closer to anarchism than to any party, argued that these settlements were not a problem but a solution. Families built gradually as their income allowed, starting with a shack and ending, twenty years later, with a solid house of several storeys, at a fraction of what the state would have spent. Housing, he said, should be understood as a verb, something people do, and not as a noun, a product delivered to them. What the poor needed from government was not houses but secure land, water, drains and the freedom to build. The World Bank adopted a version of this thinking in the 1970s, funding plots with basic services on which families built their own homes.
The Peruvian economist Hernando de Soto gave the argument a market liberal turn. In The Mystery of Capital, published in 2000, he claimed that the poor of the world already owned assets worth trillions of dollars in the form of their self-built homes, but that this was dead capital because it had no legal title. Without title they could not sell securely, borrow against the house or invest with confidence. His remedy was mass formalisation: give squatters deeds, and capitalism would work for them as it had for the West. Peru issued more than a million titles in the 1990s on his advice, and many other countries followed. Later studies found that titled families did invest more in their homes and worked more hours outside them, since they no longer had to guard the plot, but that banks remained unwilling to lend to them. Critics on the left add that title can be the first step towards displacement, since a formal plot in a well located settlement can be bought up by richer buyers.
Brazil developed the most elaborate alternative, rooted in Catholic social thought and urban social movements. Its constitution of 1988 states that property must fulfil a social function, and it allows a family that has occupied a small urban plot without challenge for five years to claim ownership. The City Statute of 2001 gave municipalities tools to act on the principle, including the power to tax vacant land at rising rates and eventually to expropriate it, and to designate zones of social interest in which informal settlements are regularised and protected from market pressure. The practical results have been uneven, but the legal idea, that ownership carries obligations and that idle property is an abuse, has spread across Latin America. From 2009 the federal government also financed several million new homes through the programme Minha Casa Minha Vida, built by private firms. Like the Chilean and Mexican schemes on which it was partly modelled, it has been criticised for placing families on cheap land far from work. In Mexico, where a payroll fund financed vast estates on the outskirts of cities in the 2000s, millions of the new houses were later abandoned by owners who could not bear the commute.
South Africa wrote the right to housing into its post-apartheid constitution, as a response to a system that had allocated land and homes by race and forcibly removed millions of people. Section 26 gives everyone the right of access to adequate housing and obliges the state to take reasonable measures, within its resources, to realise it. In the Grootboom case of 2000 the Constitutional Court held that a housing programme which made no provision for people in desperate need was unreasonable. The judgment is studied by lawyers worldwide. Irene Grootboom, the woman who brought the case, died eight years later still living in a shack, a fact often cited to show the distance between a right on paper and a house on the ground. The government has in fact delivered several million free houses since 1994, yet the number of households in informal settlements has grown, because cities have grown faster.
India and Egypt illustrate the long consequences of an earlier intervention. Bombay froze rents during the Second World War, and the freeze was made lasting by an act of 1947. Decades later, tenants in the old city were paying a few rupees a month for flats worth fortunes, owners had no reason to repair them, and buildings collapsed in the monsoon every year. New construction for rent ceased, which contributed to the growth of slums such as Dharavi, home to people with regular jobs who had nowhere else to go. Egypt froze rents under Nasser with similar effects: a large stock of decaying or deliberately empty flats in Cairo, alongside enormous informal districts built without permission on farmland. Market liberals treat both cities as textbook cases. Defenders of tenants reply that the fault lay in freezing rents without building anything, and that removing protection now would evict the elderly poor.
Running through many of these movements is a phrase coined by the French philosopher Henri Lefebvre in 1968: the right to the city. It means more than a roof. It is the claim that those who live in a city and make it what it is are entitled to remain in its centre, to use its spaces and to share in deciding its future, against a logic that would allocate each location to whoever pays most. Brazil's legislation invokes it and Ecuador's constitution of 2008 names it. Its critics find the idea too vague to guide policy. Its supporters regard it as the necessary counterweight to the view of the city as a portfolio of real estate.
Housing as a legal right
The idea that access to housing is a citizen's right, and not merely a desirable outcome, has a specific history. It belongs to the family of social and economic rights that emerged in the twentieth century alongside the older civil and political ones, and it has always been more contested than they are. Nobody disputes what the right to vote requires of the state. What a right to housing requires is far less clear.
The first constitutional statement came in Germany. The Weimar constitution of 1919 committed the state to the goal of a healthy dwelling for every German and to preventing the abuse of land. The Soviet constitution later proclaimed a right to housing outright. In the United States, Franklin Roosevelt proposed in 1944 a second Bill of Rights that would include the right of every family to a decent home, and the Housing Act of 1949 adopted the goal of a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 lists housing as part of the right to an adequate standard of living, and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights of 1966 turned that into a treaty obligation for the many states that have ratified it.
International bodies have since tried to give the right content. The United Nations committee that oversees the covenant explained in 1991 that adequate housing means more than four walls. It includes legal security of tenure, so that people are protected against arbitrary eviction, together with affordability, habitability, access to services, a location within reach of work and schools, and cultural adequacy. The committee also clarified what the right does not mean. It does not oblige the state to build a house for everyone or to provide housing free of charge. It obliges the state to work towards the goal with the resources it has, to avoid going backwards and to give priority to those in greatest need.
National constitutions vary in how seriously they take the matter. Many European and Latin American constitutions mention housing, but usually as a principle that guides legislation, not as a right an individual can enforce in court. Spain is typical. Article 47 of the constitution of 1978 declares that all Spaniards have the right to enjoy decent and adequate housing, instructs the authorities to regulate land use in the general interest so as to prevent speculation, and adds that the community shall share in the gains generated by public planning decisions, a clause with a clearly Georgist flavour. The article sits among the guiding principles of social and economic policy, however, which means a citizen without a home cannot go to a judge and demand one.
A few countries have gone further and created enforceable duties. Britain's Housing (Homeless Persons) Act of 1977 obliged local councils to secure accommodation for certain groups, mainly families with children and vulnerable people, and Scotland later extended the duty to almost everyone who is homeless through no fault of their own. France passed a law in 2007, after activists set up hundreds of tents along a canal in central Paris, which allows people waiting too long for social housing to take the state to court. In practice the courts can award damages but cannot conjure up flats, and tens of thousands of households recognised as having priority remain unhoused in the Paris region. South Africa, as described above, allows courts to review whether housing policy is reasonable. New York City has been bound since the early 1980s by a legal settlement to give shelter to anyone who asks, which has produced a very large shelter system instead of permanent homes.
The United States as a whole rejected the path. In 1972 the Supreme Court ruled that the constitution contains no right to housing of any particular quality and that the relationship between landlord and tenant is a matter for legislatures. American policy has continued to speak of housing as a goal and a need, but not as an entitlement, and only about a quarter of the households poor enough to qualify for federal rental assistance actually receive it.
The disagreement over rights is partly philosophical. For liberals in the tradition of Hayek or Robert Nozick, genuine rights are protections against interference: the right not to be robbed, censored or imprisoned without trial. A right to housing is a claim on other people's resources, and it can only be satisfied by taxing or directing someone else, which they regard as a violation of the very rights that matter. For social democrats and most human rights lawyers, the distinction does not survive scrutiny. Every right costs money to enforce, including property rights, which depend on courts, police and land registries, and a person without shelter cannot meaningfully exercise any of the classical freedoms.
The disagreement is also practical. Sceptics observe that declaring a right builds no houses, and that the countries with the grandest constitutional language are not those with the best housed populations. A right enforced against a fixed supply merely reorders the queue, moving one family ahead of another. Supporters accept that a right is not a policy, but argue that it changes what governments can get away with. It makes eviction into homelessness a legal problem and not just a private misfortune, it gives campaigners a standard against which to measure the state, and over time it shifts the burden of proof. On their account the question a society should have to answer is not why someone should be housed, but why they are not.
Where the views collide today
The traditions described above are not museum pieces. Since the financial crisis of 2008, and with greater force since the rise in rents that followed the pandemic, housing has returned to the centre of politics in almost every rich country, and the old arguments are being fought again over a handful of specific issues.
Rent control is the oldest of them. Economists distinguish the hard freezes of the wartime era from the more flexible systems adopted since the 1970s, which typically limit increases within a tenancy while allowing rents to reset between tenants or exempting new buildings. The evidence on these gentler versions is more nuanced than either side tends to admit. A much cited study of San Francisco found that rent control did what its supporters wanted, since protected tenants were considerably more likely to remain in their homes and their neighbourhood. It also did what its opponents predicted, since owners converted or redeveloped their buildings, the supply of rental housing fell, and rents in the rest of the city rose. Recent experiments repeat the pattern. Berlin froze rents in 2020 until the courts intervened. St Paul in Minnesota voted for a strict cap in 2021, saw building permits drop, and soon exempted new construction. Catalonia, Scotland and the Netherlands have all tightened controls in the past few years, with reports of lower rents for sitting tenants and of landlords selling up. How one weighs these results depends on whether the tenant in place or the tenant still searching is foremost in mind.
The short term letting of homes to tourists is a newer battleground. Platforms such as Airbnb, founded in 2008, made it simple to earn more from visitors than from a resident tenant, and in the centres of Barcelona, Lisbon, Amsterdam, Florence and New York thousands of flats left the residential market. For a market liberal this is an owner putting property to its most valued use. For residents it is the conversion of a neighbourhood into a hotel. The regulatory response has been increasingly severe. Amsterdam limits the number of nights a home may be let. New York effectively banned most short stays in 2023 unless the host is present. Barcelona announced in 2024 that it would not renew any of its roughly ten thousand tourist flat licences, intending to eliminate them by the end of 2028. Protests against mass tourism in the Canary Islands, the Balearics and Málaga in the same year were to a large extent protests about housing.
Restrictions on foreign and corporate buyers come from the same impulse. The bans and surcharges adopted in Canada, New Zealand and parts of Australia were mentioned earlier. Denmark has long limited the purchase of holiday homes by non-residents, and Spain's government proposed in 2025 a heavy tax on purchases by buyers from outside the European Union who do not live in the country. Several places have added taxes on empty dwellings, among them Vancouver, Melbourne and France. Such measures are popular across the political spectrum, although studies generally find that foreign buyers and vacant homes account for a small share of the market and that their removal changes prices only modestly. Their appeal lies in the principle they assert: that homes in a community exist first for the people who live there.
The most interesting realignment concerns building. A movement that calls itself YIMBY, for "yes in my back yard", emerged in San Francisco around 2014 among young renters who concluded that the main cause of their rents was the refusal of established homeowners to allow anything to be built. It has since influenced legislation in California, Oregon, Minnesota, New Zealand and Britain, where the Labour government elected in 2024 set a target of one and a half million homes and promised to override local objections. The movement has split both left and right. On the left, some see new private development as the solution and others see it as gentrification that displaces the poor, insisting that only non-market housing helps. On the right, libertarians who favour the freedom to build confront suburban conservatives who favour local control and the protection of existing neighbourhoods. The dividing line that matters is frequently not between parties but between those who own and those who do not.
That line increasingly follows age. In Britain, Ireland, Australia, Canada, Spain and the United States, people who bought before the long boom hold housing wealth that their children cannot match through earnings. Young adults rent for longer, live with parents for longer, form families later and inherit later. Commentators speak of a generation of renters, and some political scientists link the frustration to rising support for parties of both the radical left and the radical right among the young. Whether a young person owns a home now depends heavily on whether their parents did, which turns housing into a channel through which inequality passes from one generation to the next and undermines the conservative promise that ownership is open to anyone who works and saves.
Governments facing these pressures tend to assemble policies from every tradition at once, sometimes incoherently. A single programme may subsidise first time buyers, which is the conservative remedy, cap rents, which is the social democratic one, relax planning rules, which is the liberal one, tax empty land, which is the Georgist one, and fund cooperatives, which is the socialist one. Some of these measures work against each other. Subsidies to buyers raise the prices that affordability policies try to lower, and controls on rents may deter the building that deregulation is meant to release. The mixture reflects the underlying dilemma. Most voters want housing to be affordable for their children and want their own home to keep its value, and no government has found a way to promise both that survives arithmetic.
A country of owners: Spain as a case study
Spain deserves a closer look because nearly every tradition discussed in this essay has shaped its housing system at some point in the last seventy years, and the layers they left behind help explain why the question is so heated there today.
In 1950 Spain was a nation of tenants. More than half of households rented, and in Madrid and Barcelona the proportion was far higher. The Franco regime had frozen rents after the civil war, which protected sitting tenants but, as elsewhere, destroyed the incentive to build for rent. When millions of people left the countryside for the cities in the 1950s and 1960s, they found no housing waiting and built shanty towns on the outskirts. The regime's answer combined the conservative faith in ownership with heavy state direction. Arrese's wish for a country of owners became policy through officially protected housing, flats built by private developers with public subsidies and sold at regulated prices. Several million were built over the following decades.
The design contained a peculiarity with lasting effects. In most of northern Europe, subsidised housing remained in public or non-profit hands and was rented. In Spain it was sold, and after a set number of years its protected status expired and the owner could resell at the full market price. Public money therefore created private assets instead of a permanent public stock. By the end of the century Spain had one of the highest rates of home ownership in Western Europe, above four fifths of households, and one of the smallest social rental sectors, around two per cent of homes against several times that figure in France, Britain or the Netherlands. The rent freezes on old contracts, which lasted in modified form until the 1990s, meanwhile shrank private renting to a small fraction of the market.
Democracy added the language of rights without altering the structure. The constitution of 1978 proclaimed the right to decent housing in Article 47, as discussed above, but successive governments of both major parties continued to favour purchase through tax relief on mortgages. Then came the liberal experiment. A land law of 1998 declared that all land not specifically protected could in principle be developed, on the theory that more land would mean cheaper homes. Combined with the low interest rates that came with the euro, eager lending by regional savings banks and strong demand from immigrants and northern European buyers, the result was one of the largest property booms in history. At its height Spain was starting more than seven hundred thousand homes a year, and yet prices roughly tripled in a decade, an awkward outcome for the view that supply alone determines price when credit is abundant.
The crash that began in 2008 was correspondingly severe. Prices fell by more than a third, construction collapsed, unemployment passed a quarter of the workforce and the savings banks had to be rescued with European funds. Hundreds of thousands of families faced repossession, and under Spanish law handing back the keys did not cancel the debt, so that people lost their homes and still owed the bank. The Platform for People Affected by Mortgages, founded in Barcelona in 2009, organised neighbours to block evictions physically and became one of the most influential social movements in modern Spain. Its spokeswoman, Ada Colau, was elected mayor of Barcelona in 2015. The movement's slogans recast housing as a right in a country that had treated it for half a century as an investment.
The aftermath brought the financial investors. Banks and the public bad bank set up in 2012 sold portfolios of repossessed homes to international funds, and the city and region of Madrid sold several thousand social rental flats to such funds in 2013, a decision later challenged in the courts. Blackstone became the largest private landlord in the country. At the same time a generation that could no longer obtain mortgages turned to renting, just as tourist lets were absorbing flats in the centres of the main cities. Rents in Madrid, Barcelona, Málaga, Valencia and the islands rose far faster than wages, and the average age at which young Spaniards leave the parental home climbed to around thirty, among the highest in Europe.
The response has drawn on the social democratic tradition. Reforms in 2019 lengthened the minimum term of rental contracts. The housing law of 2023, the first national statute of its kind in the democratic period, allows regions to declare areas of market stress in which rents can be capped, limits annual increases on existing contracts, defines large landlords and places extra obligations on them, and provides that protected housing built from now on should keep its status permanently, correcting the leak that drained the public stock for decades. Application has followed party lines. Catalonia adopted the rent caps in 2024, while Madrid and other regions governed by the conservative Popular Party have declined to use them and argue instead for more building, lower taxes and legal protection for owners against illegal occupation.
Both sides can point to supporting evidence. Early data from Catalonia suggest that rents in the regulated areas have fallen or stabilised and also that the number of long term rental contracts has declined, with some owners moving to seasonal lets or selling. Large demonstrations in Madrid, Barcelona and other cities since 2024 show that the public regards the situation as an emergency. What is not in dispute is the inheritance. Spain entered the current crisis with very little non-market housing to cushion it, a population whose savings are concentrated in property, and a constitution that promises a right its institutions were never built to deliver. The asset and the right coexist in the same system and pull against each other, which makes the country a compact illustration of the whole debate.
What the disagreement is really about
After two thousand years of examples, the three questions posed at the beginning can be answered more precisely, not by settling them but by seeing what each tradition has staked on its answer.
On the purpose of a house, the market liberal says it is a good to be traded, the financier that it is a store of wealth, the conservative that it is the foundation of family and citizenship, the social democrat that it is a social right, and the socialist that it is a need which no one should profit from. Each captures something true. People do want the freedom to choose and move. They do rely on their home as security in old age. Ownership does change how people relate to their surroundings. A decent life is impossible without a secure dwelling. And the power of those who own over those who must rent is real. The traditions differ in which of these truths they are prepared to sacrifice when the truths conflict.
On land, the dividing line is whether the rise in the value of a location is treated as the owner's legitimate return or as wealth created by the community. Liberals and conservatives mostly leave it with the owner. Georgists would tax it away, socialists would take the land itself into common ownership, and the East Asian developmental states quietly kept the land and sold only the right to use it. A great deal of housing policy that is not presented as being about land turns out to depend on this choice, since the cost of building a flat differs little between cities while the cost of the ground beneath it differs enormously.
On allocation, the historical record is unkind to purists on both sides. Markets left entirely alone produced the slums of Manchester and New York, and deregulated credit produced the crashes of Tokyo, Dublin and Madrid. States that abolished the market produced the communal flat, the ten year queue and the crumbling tenement. Rent freezes preserved communities and ruined buildings. The systems that are most often admired, such as those of Vienna, Singapore, Zurich or Tokyo, have little in common ideologically. One relies on municipal landlords, one on state-built flats sold to owners, one on cooperatives and one on a permissive market. What they share is less glamorous: a steady and large supply of homes sustained over many decades, a deliberate policy about land, and enough continuity that the rules did not swing with every election.
It is also worth noticing how often the outcome of a policy has differed from the intention of its authors. Communist governments that set out to abolish property created the world's highest rates of home ownership when they fell. Conservative governments that set out to spread ownership ended by making it harder for the next generation to attain. Rent controls introduced to protect the poor helped to produce the informal settlements in which the poor now live in Mumbai and Cairo. Spain's subsidies for protected housing built private fortunes instead of a public stock. Liberal land reform intended to lower prices coincided with a tripling of them. Housing is a slow system in which buildings last a century and decisions take a generation to show their effects, and it punishes those who assume that good intentions pass straight through to results.
Beneath the ideological disagreement lies a conflict of interest that no theory removes. A policy that protects or raises the value of existing homes makes them harder for newcomers to afford. A policy that makes homes cheap reduces the wealth of those who already own. In countries where most households are owners and most of their savings are in their home, this is not a contest between a rich minority and everyone else, as nineteenth century reformers imagined, but between two large groups of ordinary people, frequently parents and their own children. Every housing ideology is in the end a decision about how to balance those two groups: whether to side with those inside the walls or those outside, and how much of the cost to ask each to bear.
This is why the two views with which the essay began, the house as financial product and the house as something close to a public good, cannot be fully reconciled and cannot be fully separated. As long as a home is the main asset of the families who own one, it will behave like an investment whatever the law declares. As long as nobody can live without one, societies will refuse to treat it as merely an investment whatever the market prefers. The ideologies surveyed here are best understood not as rival descriptions, of which one must be correct, but as different settlements between these two facts. Knowing where each settlement came from, what it achieved and what it cost is the most useful equipment a citizen can bring to an argument that every generation has to conduct again.
