The Language That Invented Itself
In the late 1970s, the government of Nicaragua opened the first school for deaf children in the country, in the capital, Managua. Until then, Nicaragua had no deaf community at all. Deaf children grew up scattered across towns and villages, isolated from one another, and each of them invented only a private set of gestures that the family around them partly understood. There was no shared sign language in the country and no adult signers who could teach one. The school changed that by accident. Nobody there set out to create a language. They simply gathered several hundred children into the same building for the first time.
The children who arrived had no common system to fall back on. Older students and younger students mixed, and something unexpected began to happen in the yard and at recess. The children started to sign to one another, borrowing gestures from their own private systems and from the signs they saw other children use, and over the following months those borrowings settled into a shared vocabulary. A child who arrived a year later found a working system already in place and learned it the way any child learns a language from the people around them. What had started as a rough, improvised mixture became something stable enough to pass on.
The most striking part came next. The youngest learners did not merely copy what they saw. They reshaped it. Compared with the older students, the children who had learned the new system from early childhood signed in a way that was more regular and more systematic. Where older signers used word order loosely and marked the relationships between ideas in several different ways, sometimes within a single sentence, the younger ones settled on one consistent order and used the same grammatical signal every time. A psychologist named Ann Senghas, who compared the two groups over many years, found that the younger signers had quietly turned a loose mixture into a system with grammar, and that the older signers eventually began to learn that grammar from the children.
Linguists who studied the community came to see two languages existing side by side. The older one, used by the first students and by adults, was rougher and less structured, closer to a makeshift mixture. The newer one, used by the students who had arrived as small children, had a full grammar: consistent sentence order, verbs that agreed with their subjects, and signs that used the space around the body to show who did what to whom. That second language had never been taught by anyone, and no adult models stood behind it. Children had assembled it, out of fragments, within a single generation.
The case of Nicaraguan Sign Language became one of the strongest pieces of evidence for an old idea in linguistics: that the human mind does not receive language passively, but builds it, and builds it most powerfully in childhood. A child exposed to a scattered, imperfect system does not reproduce the scattering. The child regularizes it. Something in the way young brains handle patterns seems to insist on structure, and once that structure exists, other children can learn it whole, and the language grows.
What happened in Managua suggests that a language is not something a community is given. It is something a community makes, and children do most of the making. Put enough young people together with a need to talk to one another, hand them nothing to talk with, and they will build the tool themselves, with a grammar that nobody taught them.
