Prospect Theory.
Prospect theory is a descriptive account of how people choose between risky options. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky introduced it in 1979 as an alternative to expected utility theory, the standard model of rational choice under risk. Their experiments showed that people systematically deviate from the predictions of that model.
Expected utility theory assumes that a decision maker evaluates each option by the expected value of its outcomes and chooses the option with the highest expected utility. Prospect theory keeps the idea of expectation but changes two central elements: how outcomes are valued and how probabilities are used.
First, people judge outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually the status quo, rather than as levels of final wealth. A change of ten dollars feels different when it is a raise and when it is a cut in pay. The reference point can shift with framing, so the same outcome may count as a gain in one description and as a loss in another.
Second, the value function is steeper for losses than for gains. This asymmetry is called loss aversion. In many experiments, a loss of a given size has about twice the psychological impact of a gain of the same size. The function is also concave for gains and convex for losses, which means that people tend to be risk averse when they face gains and risk seeking when they face losses.
Third, people do not weight probabilities linearly. Small probabilities are overweighted, which helps explain why people buy lottery tickets and insurance, while moderate and high probabilities are underweighted. Because certain outcomes are weighted especially heavily, a sure gain is often preferred to a gamble with a higher expected value. This pattern is known as the certainty effect.
The theory also describes framing effects, the reflection effect, and the isolation effect. In the reflection effect, preferences over gains reverse when the same choices are expressed as losses. The isolation effect means that people often ignore components that the options share and focus on the differences between them.
Prospect theory became the foundation of behavioral economics. Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2002; Tversky had died in 1996. Researchers have applied the theory in finance, insurance, marketing, medicine, and public policy, where loss aversion and framing shape savings, investment, and health decisions.
The original theory had limitations. It was designed to describe simple choices between two options and did not specify how decision weights combine when many outcomes are possible. Tversky and Kahneman addressed this in 1992 with cumulative prospect theory, which uses a rank-dependent method of weighting. The theory is descriptive, not normative: it says how people do choose, not how they should choose.
