The Rocket That Failed in Thirty-Seven Seconds
On the fourth of June, 1996, a new European rocket lifted off from a launch pad in French Guiana on its very first flight. It was called Ariane 5, and years of design work and billions of dollars had gone into building it. Thirty-seven seconds after liftoff, while still climbing and still well within view of the cameras on the ground, the rocket suddenly veered off its intended path, broke apart under the aerodynamic strain, and was destroyed by its own self-destruct system. The entire mission, along with four scientific satellites riding inside it, was gone in under a minute.
The cause turned out to have nothing to do with the rocket's engines, its fuel, or its structure. It came from a single piece of software, and a very small one at that. Ariane 5 reused parts of the guidance software written years earlier for its smaller predecessor, Ariane 4. Buried inside that software was a routine that converted a measurement of the rocket's horizontal velocity from a large, flexible number format into a smaller, more compact one. On the older, slower rocket, the numbers involved had always stayed safely within range. Ariane 5 was simply a faster, more powerful vehicle, and during its first seconds of flight it built up horizontal velocity quickly enough that the number overflowed the smaller format it was being squeezed into.
The software had no instructions for what to do when this happened. It did not check whether the conversion was safe, and it did not catch or recover from the resulting error. Instead, the program simply crashed, and with it went the primary system responsible for tracking the rocket's orientation. As designed, a backup system immediately took over. Unfortunately, the backup was running an identical copy of the very same software, performing the very same calculation, and it failed for the very same reason, within milliseconds of the first failure.
With both guidance systems down, the rocket's main flight computer received a stream of garbled diagnostic data left behind by the crashed software and, having no way to tell that this data was meaningless, treated it as a legitimate description of the rocket's position. Believing the rocket was wildly off course when it was not, the flight computer commanded an extreme and unnecessary correction. The aerodynamic forces produced by that sudden maneuver tore the vehicle apart almost instantly.
Investigators later noted a detail that made the failure even harder to accept: the specific piece of code that overflowed was not even needed after liftoff. It had been included to serve a purpose relevant only before launch, and nobody had reexamined whether it still needed to run once the rocket left the ground. A four-billion-dollar rocket had been destroyed by an unnecessary calculation that nobody had thought to question.
