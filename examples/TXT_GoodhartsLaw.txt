Goodhart's Law
Goodhart's law is the observation that when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The idea is named after Charles Goodhart, a British economist who set it out in a 1975 paper on monetary policy. His original statement was technical: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. Goodhart had been studying the supply of money in Britain, and the stable relationship he described held only while nobody tried to exploit it. When the Bank of England began to steer policy by those figures, the connections weakened and the numbers stopped meaning what they had meant.
The familiar wording, that a measure which becomes a target ceases to be a good measure, comes from the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, who used it in 1997 in a paper about audit in British universities. Goodhart himself thought the popular version distorted his point, because it turned a narrow claim about statistical prediction into a broad warning about incentives. Both readings share a core idea. A measure is a proxy for something we value, and a proxy stays honest only while the thing behind it is not being pushed directly.
The pattern appears wherever people are rewarded for a number. French officials in Hanoi offered a bounty for every rat killed in 1902, and they paid for a rat's tail; hunters began to leave the bodies in the streets and to farm the animals for their tails. A much-repeated story says that Soviet planners who ordered nails by weight received enormous ones and, when they switched to counting nails, received tiny ones. Tales of bounties on cobras and of quota-driven absurdity are told so often that their details are hard to verify, yet the logic they describe is easy to recognize.
Modern institutions supply plenty of examples. Sales targets at Wells Fargo led employees to open millions of accounts that customers had not asked for. Volkswagen engineers installed software that cheated on emissions tests instead of meeting the standards. Schools judged by test scores narrowed their teaching, and in Atlanta administrators altered answer sheets. Hospitals given waiting-time targets found ways to record the clock differently. In each case the number improved while the purpose it was meant to serve did not.
The mechanism is not a single phenomenon. A 2018 paper by David Manheim and Scott Garrabrant sorts the failures into four kinds. In the regressional kind the statistical link between the proxy and the goal weakens at the edges of the observed range. In the extremal kind the push carries the system beyond the range where the measure was ever tested. In the causal kind the measure is a result of the goal rather than a cause of it, so forcing the number does not move what matters. In the adversarial kind people deliberately manipulate the measure. The social scientist Donald Campbell described a close relative of the law in 1979, warning that any social indicator used for decisions becomes corrupted by the pressure it invites. Economists know a similar problem as the Lucas critique, since a change in policy changes the behavior that earlier data described.
Goodhart's law is not an argument against measurement. It is a warning about the moment a diagnostic tool is turned into a target, and about the confidence that numbers acquire once careers depend on them. The usual defenses are modest: use several measures instead of one, rotate them before they are gamed, check that a measure still tracks the goal, and leave room for the judgment of the people doing the work. A figure that is watched closely will always drift, and the remedy is to watch the purpose as well.
