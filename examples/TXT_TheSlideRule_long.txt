The Slide Rule
For about three hundred and fifty years, the most important calculating machine in science and engineering was a ruler that slid inside another ruler. It had no gears, no power source and no memory. It simply turned the hard problem of multiplication into the easy problem of adding two lengths.
The slide rule helped design steam engines, bridges, aircraft and the rockets that reached the Moon. Then, within a single decade, it vanished from desks and shirt pockets almost completely. Its story is worth telling both for what the instrument did and for what it demanded of the people who used it.
From logarithms to sliding scales
The slide rule began as an idea rather than an object. In 1614 the Scottish mathematician John Napier published his tables of logarithms, which rest on one powerful property: the logarithm of a product equals the sum of the logarithms of its factors. Anyone with a table could now replace a long multiplication with a short addition.
Around 1620 the English astronomer Edmund Gunter engraved those logarithms onto a ruler. On Gunter's line, the distance from 1 to any number was proportional to that number's logarithm. A navigator could multiply by stepping off distances with a pair of dividers.
A couple of years later the clergyman and mathematician William Oughtred saw that the dividers were unnecessary. Two logarithmic scales laid side by side and slid against each other would do the adding on their own. Oughtred made both straight and circular versions, and with them the slide rule proper was born.
The form most people recognise came much later. In the 1850s a French artillery officer, Amédée Mannheim, standardised the arrangement of scales and popularised the cursor, the sliding runner with a hairline that carries a value from one scale to another. By the end of the nineteenth century, duplex rules with scales on both faces were being made in large numbers.
Adding lengths to multiply numbers
A slide rule works because its scales are spaced by logarithm rather than by value. The gap between 1 and 2 is the same as the gap between 2 and 4, or between 4 and 8, since each step represents the same ratio.
To multiply 2 by 3, the user slides the 1 of the moving scale over the 2 of the fixed scale. Under the 3 of the moving scale, the fixed scale then reads 6. Two lengths have been laid end to end, two logarithms have been added, and the product appears with no arithmetic at all. Division is the same movement in reverse.
Further scales extended the principle. Squares and cubes, roots, reciprocals, sines and tangents all sat a hairline apart on a strip about 25 centimetres long. The better rules added log log scales, which raise any number to any power.
Two things the slide rule would not do. It could not add or subtract, and it never told the user where the decimal point belonged. A reading of 6 might mean 0.06, 6 or 6,000, and deciding which was the operator's job.
The engineer's instrument
From the industrial revolution to the space age, the slide rule was the working tool of anyone who calculated for a living. James Watt and Matthew Boulton had rules made for their engine works at Soho in the late eighteenth century. Specialised versions followed for excise officers, carpenters, artillerymen, chemists and electricians.
By the twentieth century it was mass produced in mahogany, celluloid, bamboo, aluminium and plastic. Firms such as Keuffel & Esser and Pickett in the United States, Faber-Castell, Aristo and Nestler in Germany, and Hemmi in Japan sold millions. For generations of students, buying a good rule was a rite of entry into the profession, and the case on the belt marked an engineer as clearly as a stethoscope marked a doctor.
Its reach is easy to underestimate. The Golden Gate Bridge, the Spitfire, the first jet engines, the early nuclear reactors and the Saturn V were all designed largely with three figure arithmetic worked by hand. A small Pickett rule flew on the Apollo missions as a backup, and Buzz Aldrin took one on Apollo 11.
What it taught
The slide rule's limits were also its lessons. A standard rule gives about three significant figures, and no amount of squinting produces a fourth. Users learned early that an answer is only as good as the measurements behind it, and that quoting more digits than the data supports is a kind of dishonesty.
Because the instrument ignored the decimal point, every calculation began with a rough estimate made in the head. An engineer who expected a beam load near 4,000 kilograms would not accept 40,000 from a slipped scale. That habit of knowing the size of the answer before computing it is what people mean by a feel for numbers.
The rule also made mathematics visible. Ratios appeared as distances and proportions as alignments, and a whole family of equivalent fractions could be read along a single setting of the slide. Logarithms stopped being an abstraction and became something a student could feel under the thumb.
The sudden end
Few technologies have been displaced as quickly. In 1972 Hewlett-Packard launched the HP-35, the first pocket calculator with trigonometric and logarithmic functions, at a price of 395 dollars. It returned ten digits at the press of a key, and its maker advertised it openly as an electronic slide rule.
Prices then collapsed. Texas Instruments released the SR-50 in 1974, its initials standing for slide rule, and within a few years a scientific calculator cost less than a good rule. By the middle of the decade the major manufacturers had stopped production, and schools dropped the instrument from their teaching soon after.
Nothing about the slide rule had failed. It was as accurate and reliable in 1976 as it had been in 1876. It was simply outclassed in speed, precision and ease, all at once, by a device that asked nothing of its user.
What remains
The slide rule survives in corners. Pilots still train with the E6B flight computer, a circular slide rule for fuel, time and wind problems that needs no batteries. Some watches carry a rotating logarithmic bezel, and collectors keep the finest rules in working order.
Its deeper legacy is an attitude. The slide rule assumed that the person holding it understood the problem, could estimate the result and would know a wrong answer on sight. Modern tools assume none of that. They are far more powerful, but the judgement they no longer demand is still what separates engineering from mere calculation.
That is why the slide rule deserves to be remembered as more than an obsolete gadget. It was a partnership, three centuries long, between a simple mathematical idea and a trained human mind. For all that time, the mind did at least half of the work.
