# How Brands Grow by Byron Sharp: An Essay

Oct 31, 2026 · @Miguel Puche

## Introduction: Laws Instead of Opinions

How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know, published by Oxford University Press in 2010, set out to do something unusual in marketing literature: to replace opinion, anecdote and fashionable theory with regularities drawn from decades of data on how people actually buy things. Its author, Byron Sharp, is a professor of marketing science and the director of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute for Marketing Science at the University of South Australia in Adelaide. The book's tone is combative from the first page. Sharp argues that much of what marketers believe about loyalty, differentiation, targeting and advertising is not supported by evidence, and that the discipline has been run on myths for too long. He presents his alternative not as another framework but as a set of empirical laws, comparable to the regularities that physical sciences rely on.

The intellectual history behind the book is essential to understanding it. The Institute takes its name from two researchers. Andrew Ehrenberg, a British statistician who worked for many years at the London Business School and later at London South Bank University, spent half a century analysing consumer panel data, beginning with work in the late 1950s showing that repeat purchasing followed a predictable statistical pattern known as the negative binomial distribution. With Gerald Goodhardt and Christopher Chatfield he later developed the Dirichlet model, which predicts with surprising accuracy how many people will buy a brand, how often, and which other brands they will also buy, using little more than each brand's market share and the category's purchase rate. Frank Bass, an American marketing scientist, is famous for the Bass diffusion model, which describes how new products are adopted over time. The Institute adopted both names in the mid 2000s, and Sharp's book is in many ways a popularization of the Ehrenberg tradition for practitioners.

The story of the book's reception is a story of slow conversion. At first it circulated mainly among marketing scientists and a minority of practitioners attracted by its contrarian claims. Over the following decade it gained an unusually committed following among brand managers, media planners and marketing commentators, and it became one of the most discussed marketing books of its era. A sequel, How Brands Grow Part 2, written by Jenni Romaniuk and Byron Sharp and published in 2016, extended the analysis to emerging markets, luxury goods, durables and services, and Romaniuk's 2018 book Building Distinctive Brand Assets developed one of the core ideas in practical detail.

On the surface, How Brands Grow is a book about marketing metrics and brand strategy, not manufacturing. Yet its arguments have direct implications for production, logistics and distribution, because one of its two central concepts, physical availability, is fundamentally a supply chain idea. Sharp argues that brands grow by being easy to buy as well as easy to think of, and that the commercial importance of distribution, shelf presence, stock availability, pack formats and retail coverage has been systematically underestimated by marketers. This essay traces each of the book's main laws and concepts, paying particular attention to what they mean for the people who make, move and stock products, before turning to the criticism the book attracted and its influence on industry.

## Double Jeopardy

The first and most famous law in How Brands Grow is double jeopardy. The term was coined by the sociologist William McPhee in the early 1960s, in a study of the popularity of radio presenters and comic strips. McPhee noticed that less popular presenters suffered twice: not only were they known by fewer people, but the people who did know them also liked them somewhat less than the fans of popular presenters liked theirs. Ehrenberg and his colleagues later found the same pattern in brand purchase data across a very wide range of categories. Brands with smaller market share have far fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal, purchasing the brand a little less often than buyers of larger brands purchase theirs.

The key insight is about the relative size of the two effects. Sharp shows that differences in market share between competing brands come overwhelmingly from differences in the number of buyers, known as penetration, and only modestly from differences in how often those buyers purchase, known as purchase frequency or loyalty. A brand with twice the market share of a rival typically has nearly twice as many buyers, while its average buyer purchases only slightly more often. In data tables reproduced throughout the book, purchase frequency across brands in a category tends to be strikingly similar, while penetration varies enormously.

This finding overturns a common assumption in marketing practice. Many strategies are built on the belief that a brand can grow by deepening the loyalty of its existing customers, getting them to buy more often or to spend more. Double jeopardy suggests that this is rarely how growth happens. Big brands are big because many people buy them, not because a small group buys them obsessively. To grow, a brand must increase penetration, which means reaching and recruiting more buyers, most of whom buy the category only occasionally.

For production and supply chain planning, double jeopardy has a practical meaning. Growth in volume for an established brand usually comes from a broader customer base rather than from heavier consumption by existing customers. A broader base means more points of sale, more geographic coverage, more varied purchase occasions and often more pack formats. The operational challenge of growth is therefore primarily a distribution challenge: getting the product into more places where more people can encounter it. A manufacturer planning for growth should expect to invest in coverage and availability rather than assuming that existing customers will simply buy more through existing channels.

Double jeopardy also helps explain why small brands find it so hard to grow. They suffer from lower penetration and slightly lower loyalty, and their smaller volume often means weaker distribution, less shelf space and higher unit costs in production and logistics. The pattern is self reinforcing. Sharp's analysis implies that small brands should not expect a loyal niche to carry them to scale, and that their path to growth runs through the same mechanisms as for large brands: reaching more buyers and being available to them.

Sharp presents double jeopardy as a law because of its consistency. It appears in packaged goods, in services such as banking and insurance, in durables such as cars, and in media audiences. The regularity is not perfect, and the book discusses deviations, such as niche brands that have slightly higher loyalty than their size would predict, or change of pace brands that have lower loyalty because people buy them for variety. But the deviations are small and explicable, and the general pattern holds remarkably widely.

## Retention, Loyalty and the Leaky Bucket

A second set of arguments in How Brands Grow concerns customer retention and loyalty programmes, which were at the height of their fashion when the book was written. During the 1990s and 2000s, a widely repeated management claim held that it costs several times more to acquire a new customer than to retain an existing one, and that small improvements in retention could produce very large increases in profit. Loyalty cards, frequent flyer schemes, customer relationship management software and retention departments proliferated on the back of this belief. Sharp challenges it directly.

His first observation is that all brands lose customers every year, in what he describes with the image of a leaky bucket. Customers move house, change their circumstances, try a competitor or simply drift away. The rate of loss is not primarily a sign of marketing failure. It follows, like loyalty itself, the double jeopardy pattern: larger brands lose a smaller proportion of their customers each year than smaller brands, but the differences are modest and predictable from market share. A brand cannot easily reduce its defection rate far below what its size would predict, because most defection is not caused by dissatisfaction but by the normal churn of life and of competitive markets.

The second observation is that loyalty in most categories is divided, not exclusive. The Dirichlet model and the panel data behind it show that most buyers of a brand also buy competing brands, and that they do so routinely. A buyer of one soft drink brand typically buys several others over a year. In repertoire markets such as groceries and toiletries, the heavily loyal, single brand customer that loyalty programmes imagine is a small minority. Even in subscription markets, where customers are formally attached to one supplier at a time, such as banking or insurance, switching and multiple holding are common over time.

From these observations Sharp concludes that retention strategies are limited in what they can achieve. Loyalty programmes mainly reward people who were already the brand's heavier buyers, giving them discounts on purchases they would have made anyway. They rarely change the brand's loyalty metrics beyond what its market share predicts. Since growth comes mainly from penetration, a strategy that concentrates resources on existing customers leaves the brand vulnerable to the steady leak of buyers through the bottom of the bucket. To grow, a brand must pour in new buyers faster than it loses them, and that requires reaching the much larger group of light buyers and non buyers.

There are operational implications here as well. Loyalty programmes require substantial infrastructure: data systems, point of sale integration, rewards fulfilment and customer service. In retail and in consumer goods, they also influence how promotions are designed and how stock flows through stores. Sharp's argument suggests that the money and operational effort spent on these systems might often be better spent on extending the brand's reach and availability, for instance by improving distribution coverage, reducing out of stock rates or developing pack formats suited to new channels.

The leaky bucket idea also has a useful analogue for supply chain thinking. Just as a brand loses customers at a predictable baseline rate, it loses sales every time the product is not available where a buyer looks for it. A customer who finds an empty shelf may switch to a competitor for that occasion, and because loyalty is divided, many buyers will do so without hesitation. Every stock out therefore acts as a small leak. Sharp's emphasis on availability makes clear that service levels in the supply chain are not merely a cost question but a direct determinant of market share.

## Pareto Revisited and the Importance of Light Buyers

Marketers have long invoked the Pareto principle, the rule of thumb that roughly eighty percent of sales come from twenty percent of customers. If that were true, a brand could concentrate its efforts on a small group of heavy buyers and largely ignore the rest. Sharp examined this claim against panel data and found that the real ratio is far less extreme. In typical consumer goods categories, the top twenty percent of a brand's buyers account for something closer to half or a little more of its sales, not eighty percent. The remaining eighty percent of buyers, most of whom purchase the brand only once or twice a year, contribute the other half or so.

The reason lies in the shape of purchase frequency distributions. In any period, the buyers of a brand range from a few heavy users to a very large number of people who buy it rarely. The negative binomial distribution that Ehrenberg described captures this pattern: many people buy once, fewer buy twice, fewer still buy three times, and so on, with a long tail of heavy buyers. Moreover, over a longer period, the composition of the heavy group changes. Some of this year's heavy buyers will be lighter next year and some light buyers will become heavier, a statistical effect known as regression to the mean. Targeting the heavy buyers of one year therefore captures a moving and partly random group.

The practical message is that light buyers matter enormously. They are the majority of a brand's customer base, they account for a large share of its sales, and, as double jeopardy shows, they are where growth comes from. Many light buyers are also light buyers of the category, people who seldom think about the product at all. For a brand to grow, it must reach these people and be present in their minds and in their path when the rare buying occasion arrives.

This has significant implications for media planning, which Sharp develops later in the book, but it also matters for operations. Light buyers do not shop in the same way as heavy enthusiasts. They are less likely to go out of their way to find a brand, less likely to order it specially, and more likely to buy whatever is easily available at the moment of need. A heavy buyer may visit a specialist store or wait for a delivery; a light buyer will pick up whatever is on the shelf in front of them. That is why broad distribution, wide channel coverage and high in stock rates are so important for growth. The supply chain must serve the occasional buyer at the moment of purchase, wherever that may be.

The light buyer argument also helps explain the economics of pack sizes and formats. Heavy buyers may prefer large packs and multi packs, which offer lower unit prices. Light buyers often prefer single servings or small packs, sold in convenience stores, petrol stations, vending machines or online. A brand that offers only the formats favoured by its heavy buyers will struggle to reach light buyers. Manufacturing and packaging capabilities that support a range of formats, and logistics networks that reach a wide variety of outlets, therefore become strategic assets in Sharp's framework.

The reassessment of Pareto is one of the clearest examples of the book's method. Sharp takes a widely held belief, tests it against large volumes of purchase data from multiple countries and categories, and shows that the belief is exaggerated. The conclusion is not that heavy buyers are unimportant, but that they are less important than marketers assumed, and that strategies built on the eighty twenty myth systematically neglect the customers on whom growth depends.

## Duplication of Purchase and Market Partitions

The duplication of purchase law describes how customers are shared between competing brands. If one looks at the buyers of any brand and asks what proportion of them also bought each competing brand during the same period, a strong regularity appears. A brand shares customers with other brands roughly in proportion to those brands' penetration. A large competitor shares many customers with every other brand, simply because it has many buyers; a small competitor shares few. Ehrenberg and colleagues showed that a single coefficient can predict these sharing patterns across a whole category with considerable accuracy.

The law has a striking implication: in most categories, brands do not have distinct customer bases. The people who buy one brand look very much like the people who buy its competitors, in demographics, attitudes and behaviour. Sharp argues that this contradicts much of the conventional wisdom about segmentation and positioning, which assumes that brands appeal to different kinds of people. In reality, he contends, brands compete for the same buyers, and each brand's customers are largely a sample of the category's customers as a whole.

Duplication of purchase is also a tool for identifying where markets do have structure. When two brands share noticeably more or fewer customers than the law predicts, it signals a partition in the market. Partitions usually arise from functional differences rather than image. In detergents, for example, liquid and powder formats may form partitions, because people who use liquid tend to buy liquid brands. In cars, size class or price tier may create partitions. In beverages, diet and regular variants may partition the market. These partitions are typically driven by physical product characteristics, formats or price points, which means they are rooted in product design and manufacturing decisions.

This gives the law a direct relevance to product and range planning. A manufacturer deciding whether to introduce a new format, size or variant can use duplication analysis to understand which competitors that variant will draw customers from. If the new variant sits in a different partition, it may open up a group of buyers the brand currently does not reach; if it sits in the same partition, it will mainly compete with the brand's existing products and with the large brands that dominate the category. Range decisions, which have significant implications for production complexity, changeover costs and inventory, can therefore be grounded in evidence rather than intuition.

The duplication law also clarifies who a brand's real competitors are. Because a brand shares most customers with the biggest brands in its category, its sales are most exposed to the actions of those big competitors, regardless of how it is positioned. For supply chain planning, this suggests that competitive moves by large brands, such as heavy promotions, new formats or distribution gains, will have predictable spillover effects on smaller brands' volumes. A smaller brand cannot assume that it is insulated by a distinct niche.

Finally, the law reinforces Sharp's argument against narrow targeting. If a brand's buyers are much like everyone else's, then aiming marketing only at a tightly defined segment will miss most of the brand's potential customers. The same logic applies to distribution. Restricting a brand to a narrow set of channels because they suit an imagined target customer will limit its reach among the very broad population that, in practice, buys the category.

## Mental Availability

Having shown that brands grow mainly by increasing penetration, Sharp asks what makes more people buy a brand. His answer rests on two concepts: mental availability and physical availability. A brand grows by becoming easier to think of and easier to buy, across more buying situations, for more people. These two ideas form the strategic core of the book, and together they replace the traditional emphasis on persuasion, differentiation and loyalty building.

Mental availability refers to the likelihood that a brand comes to mind when a buyer is in a buying situation. It draws on research in memory and cognitive psychology, which shows that the brain stores information in networks of associations. A brand is more likely to be recalled if it is linked to many relevant cues and if those links are fresh and strong. Sharp, and later Jenni Romaniuk in more detail, emphasize that what matters is not a single brand image or a unique selling proposition, but the breadth of a brand's links to the situations in which people buy. Romaniuk later called these situations category entry points: the needs, occasions, places, times and moods that trigger category purchase, such as being thirsty after exercise, needing a gift, hosting friends or running out at home.

In this framework, advertising's main job is not to persuade people with arguments but to refresh and build memory structures, so that the brand is noticed and recalled when the buying moment arrives. Most buyers are light buyers who think about the category rarely and give advertising little attention. Advertising must therefore be easily processed, clearly branded and seen by as many category buyers as possible, repeatedly over time. Sharp argues that much advertising fails not because its message is weak but because viewers do not correctly identify which brand it is for.

Mental availability might seem far removed from manufacturing, but the two are connected in several ways. Product design, packaging and physical appearance contribute to the memory structures that make a brand recognizable. The shape of a bottle, the colour of a pack, the design of a logo on a product, and even the sound or texture of a product in use can all act as cues that trigger recall. A manufacturer that frequently changes packaging designs, or that allows its product to look like competitors', weakens the brand's mental availability. Consistency in pack design across formats, markets and production runs thus becomes a marketing asset.

Mental availability also shapes where and when a product should be sold. Because category entry points are the situations in which people think of buying, a brand that wants to be linked to an entry point must often be physically present in the corresponding situation. A beverage linked to sports should be available at gyms and stadiums; a snack linked to commuting should be sold in stations and convenience stores. In this way the mental and physical sides of availability reinforce each other, and decisions about channels and distribution become part of building memory.

Sharp's emphasis on reach and continuity, discussed later in the book, follows from the logic of mental availability. Memories fade, so brands must refresh them constantly. Brands that go dark, cutting advertising for long periods, gradually lose mental availability and, eventually, sales. This argument, supported by later analyses of advertising effectiveness, challenged the habit of treating advertising as a discretionary cost that could be cut when budgets tightened.

## Physical Availability: The Logistics of Growth

If mental availability is about being easy to think of, physical availability is about being easy to buy. Sharp defines it broadly as the ease with which a brand can be found and purchased, across as many buying situations as possible. This is the part of How Brands Grow that speaks most directly to manufacturing, logistics and distribution, and it is arguably the most practically important idea in the book. Sharp's contention is that marketers have long treated distribution as a dull operational matter, delegated to sales teams and supply chain departments, when in fact it is one of the most powerful drivers of market share.

The argument begins with the observation that most buyers are light buyers with divided loyalty. They do not search hard for any particular brand. When they enter a store or open a shopping app, they choose among the options in front of them, and they will happily buy a competitor if their usual brand is missing, hard to find or unavailable in the right size. For such buyers, a brand that is not physically present at the moment of purchase effectively does not exist. A large share of brand growth therefore depends on simply being there.

Sharp and his colleagues break physical availability into several components, which Romaniuk and Sharp later summarized as presence, relevance and prominence. Presence refers to the breadth of distribution: how many outlets, channels, regions and platforms carry the brand, and how many hours a day it can be bought. Relevance refers to whether the brand is offered in forms that suit the buying situation, such as the right pack sizes, flavours, price points and formats for each channel. Prominence refers to how easy the brand is to notice and find at the point of purchase, through shelf position, number of facings, displays, packaging that stands out and, online, search rankings and page placement.

Each of these components maps onto concrete operational decisions. Presence depends on route to market design, the network of distributors and wholesalers, the agreements with retail chains, the coverage of the sales force and the capacity of the logistics network to serve many outlets economically. In many emerging markets, which How Brands Grow Part 2 examines, this means reaching millions of small independent shops and kiosks, which requires dense distribution networks, frequent small deliveries and products designed for small retailers with little storage space. In developed markets it increasingly means omnichannel availability: supermarkets, discounters, convenience stores, forecourts, vending, e-commerce and quick commerce platforms.

Relevance depends on the product range and the manufacturing capability behind it. A brand that wants to be bought at a petrol station needs a single serve format; one that wants to be bought for family stocking up needs a large pack or multi pack; one that wants to sell in low income markets may need sachets or small packs at very low price points. Each format has implications for production lines, packaging materials, filling equipment, palletization and transport efficiency. Physical availability, in this sense, starts in the factory, with the ability to produce the formats that different channels and occasions require.

Prominence depends on retail execution and packaging. Shelf space, the number of facings, eye level placement, secondary displays and promotional ends are negotiated with retailers and maintained by merchandising teams. Planograms, the diagrams that define where each product sits on a shelf, become strategic documents. Packaging design contributes by making the brand easy to spot, which links prominence back to the distinctive assets that build mental availability. Online, prominence depends on product data, images, search optimization and the algorithms of retail platforms, so the digital shelf becomes a new frontier of physical availability.

A fourth dimension, often discussed by practitioners inspired by the book, is reliability: the product must actually be on the shelf when the buyer arrives. Retail research has consistently found that on shelf availability in grocery is far from perfect, with a noticeable share of items out of stock at any moment, and that a substantial proportion of shoppers who face a stock out buy a competing brand or delay or abandon the purchase. In Sharp's framework, every stock out is a lost opportunity to reach a light buyer and, potentially, a gift to a competitor. Forecast accuracy, replenishment frequency, warehouse performance and the coordination between manufacturer and retailer thus have a direct effect on market share.

The book also encourages marketers to measure physical availability with the same seriousness as advertising. Traditional distribution metrics, such as numeric distribution, the share of stores that stock a product, and weighted distribution, the share of category sales accounted for by those stores, become key indicators of brand health. Sharp's argument implies that gains in weighted distribution often produce sales growth more reliably than changes in positioning or messaging, and that losses of distribution can quietly erode a brand even when its advertising is strong.

For supply chain professionals, this chapter of How Brands Grow provides a powerful language for arguing the commercial value of their work. Investments in network coverage, service levels, packaging flexibility and retail execution are not merely operational costs; they are investments in brand growth, as fundamental as advertising. Conversely, cost cutting programmes that reduce the number of outlets served, rationalize formats too aggressively or allow service levels to slip may save money in the short term while eroding the physical availability on which market share depends.

## Distinctiveness Versus Differentiation

One of the most provocative claims in How Brands Grow is that differentiation, long considered the cornerstone of brand strategy, is overrated. Classic marketing theory, from the unique selling proposition of the 1950s to the positioning school of the 1980s, held that a brand must offer something meaningfully different from its competitors and communicate that difference clearly. Sharp argues that, in practice, most consumers see little meaningful difference between competing brands, and that brands within a category are bought by similar people for similar reasons. When customers are asked what makes their chosen brand different, many cannot say, and the attributes they associate with a brand tend to scale with the brand's size rather than revealing distinct positions.

Instead of differentiation, Sharp emphasizes distinctiveness. A distinctive brand is one that is easy to recognize and identify, not necessarily one that is meaningfully different. Distinctive brand assets include colours, logos, shapes, characters, typefaces, sounds, slogans and other sensory cues that people associate uniquely with the brand. Their job is to help buyers notice and recognize the brand quickly, in advertising and at the point of sale. Coca-Cola's red and its contour bottle, the golden arches of McDonald's and the purple of Cadbury are frequently cited examples of the kind of asset Sharp has in mind.

The distinction has profound implications for packaging and product design, which are core manufacturing concerns. If distinctive assets are vital for both mental and physical availability, then packaging is not just a container but a carrier of brand memory. Changing the shape, colour or graphic system of a pack can damage the brand's recognizability, even if the new design is more attractive or more efficient to produce. Several well known redesigns that confused customers and were later reversed illustrate the risk. In Sharp's framework, packaging consistency is a strategic asset that operations and procurement teams must protect when they consider changes in materials, suppliers or formats.

The same logic applies to product form. A distinctive product shape, such as a particular bottle silhouette or the form of a chocolate bar, is a manufacturing feature that also functions as a brand asset. Protecting it may require investment in specialized moulds, tooling or production processes that a cost focused manufacturer might be tempted to standardize. Conversely, when new packaging technologies or sustainability goals require changes, such as moving from glass to plastic, from plastic to paper or reducing material weight, the brand's distinctive cues need to be carried over carefully so that recognition is not lost.

Sharp's critique of differentiation does not mean that product quality or functional innovation is irrelevant. Products must be good enough to satisfy buyers and to stand comparison with competitors, and functional differences can create market partitions, as the duplication of purchase law shows. But he argues that most brands do not grow by convincing people that they are uniquely superior. They grow by being easy to recognize, easy to recall and easy to buy, while being comparable in quality to their rivals.

This argument placed How Brands Grow in direct tension with books such as Purple Cow, which insist that being remarkably different is the key to growth. The tension can be partly reconciled by noting that distinctiveness and meaningful difference are not mutually exclusive, and that a remarkable product feature can also become a distinctive asset. But the emphasis is clearly different: where Godin asks how a product can be extraordinary, Sharp asks how a brand can be unmistakable and everywhere.

## Price Promotions and Their Operational Costs

How Brands Grow devotes substantial attention to price promotions, which in many consumer goods companies absorb a larger share of the marketing budget than advertising. Sharp summarizes research showing that price promotions produce large, immediate spikes in sales, but that these spikes come mostly from the brand's existing buyers, who stock up or bring forward purchases they would have made anyway. Promotions attract relatively few genuinely new buyers, and the ones they do attract tend to return to their usual repertoire afterwards. When the promotion ends, sales usually return to their previous baseline, and sometimes dip below it as buyers use up the stock they accumulated.

The conclusion is that most price promotions do not build brands. They do not create lasting increases in penetration or loyalty, and many of them lose money once the cost of the discount is weighed against the incremental volume. Sharp also notes that frequent promotions can train buyers to wait for discounts and can erode the price at which a brand is perceived. Promotions may have tactical uses, for example to secure displays or to respond to competitors, but they are a poor substitute for building mental and physical availability.

The operational consequences of this analysis are substantial, and here the book's argument connects with a well established body of supply chain research. Price promotions create sharp, artificial swings in demand. A product that normally sells at a steady rate may sell several times its usual volume during a promotional week and then fall below normal in the weeks that follow. These swings must be absorbed by manufacturing and logistics: production must be increased in advance, warehouses must hold extra stock, transport must handle peaks, and retailers must find space for promotional volumes.

Supply chain scholars have long identified promotions as a major cause of the bullwhip effect, the amplification of demand variability as it moves upstream from consumers to retailers, distributors and manufacturers. In an influential 1997 article, Hau Lee, V. Padmanabhan and Seungjin Whang described how retailer and distributor ordering patterns can fluctuate far more than consumer demand, and they identified price fluctuations and trade promotions as one of the key causes. When manufacturers offer temporary discounts to retailers, retailers buy ahead, building stock at the discounted price and then ordering little for weeks afterwards. Manufacturers see a pattern of feast and famine that bears little relation to actual consumption, which drives up costs for capacity, overtime, inventory and transport.

Sharp's evidence that promotions rarely build brands strengthens the economic case against excessive promotional activity. If promotions do not generate lasting sales growth, and if they impose significant costs through supply chain volatility, waste and forecasting errors, then their true cost is even higher than the discount itself. The book therefore implicitly supports strategies such as everyday low pricing, steadier promotional calendars and closer collaboration between manufacturers and retailers on demand planning, all of which reduce volatility and allow supply chains to run more efficiently.

This is an area where marketing and operations interests align closely. Marketing teams under pressure to deliver short term volume often favour promotions, while supply chain teams bear the consequences of the resulting variability. How Brands Grow offers both sides a shared evidence base. Reallocating spending from deep, frequent discounts to reach based advertising and to improvements in distribution and availability would, by Sharp's analysis, produce more durable growth while also smoothing the demand that factories and warehouses must serve.

## Advertising Reach and Continuity

Sharp's prescriptions for advertising follow directly from his laws. Because brands grow by increasing penetration, and because the people who must be reached are mostly light buyers who rarely think about the category, advertising should aim for broad reach across all category buyers rather than narrow targeting of heavy users or tightly defined segments. Sharp describes the goal as sophisticated mass marketing: using modern tools and data, but with the objective of reaching as many potential buyers as possible, as cost effectively as possible.

Continuity is the second principle. Memory structures decay, and buying occasions are spread throughout the year, so a brand that advertises only in short, intense bursts will be absent from many people's minds when they come to buy. Sharp advocates spreading advertising over time to maintain a continuous presence, rather than concentrating it in a few flights. This contradicts the common practice of heavy launches followed by long silences and supports the view that advertising should be treated as an ongoing investment in maintaining the brand's mental availability.

The third principle concerns creative execution. Advertising must be noticed and correctly attributed to the brand, which means making heavy use of distinctive brand assets and avoiding executions in which the brand is an afterthought. Sharp points to research showing that a large share of advertising is not correctly linked to the advertiser by those who see it, which wastes a significant part of media investment. Clear branding is, in this view, more important than clever messaging.

These prescriptions were reinforced by subsequent work from other researchers, notably Les Binet and Peter Field, whose analyses of the IPA advertising effectiveness databank, published in The Long and the Short of It in 2013, concluded that long term brand building through broad, emotionally engaging campaigns tends to generate greater profit growth than short term activation alone, and that budgets should usually be balanced with a substantial share devoted to brand building. Together, Sharp's laws and Binet and Field's analyses formed an evidence base that many practitioners used to argue against the drift toward narrowly targeted digital performance marketing in the 2010s.

For manufacturers, reach and continuity have an indirect but real operational significance. Steady, continuous advertising tends to produce more stable demand than intense bursts, which supports smoother production planning. Broad reach advertising, aimed at bringing in light buyers across the population, is most effective when it is matched by broad distribution, so that newly prompted buyers can find the product wherever they shop. Sharp's model implies that advertising and distribution must be planned together: there is little value in reaching millions of light buyers with advertising if the product is absent from the stores where they buy.

The argument also has implications for launches. When a new product or variant is introduced, the supply chain must ensure that it reaches broad distribution before or as advertising begins, otherwise advertising spending is wasted and early buyers meet empty shelves. Coordination between commercial launch plans and supply readiness, including initial stock building and the pipeline fill to distributors and retailers, is therefore central to turning advertising into sales.

## Portfolio, Range and Manufacturing Implications

Although How Brands Grow focuses on individual brands, its laws have consequences for how companies manage portfolios of brands and product ranges, and these consequences fall heavily on manufacturing. A recurring theme is that a brand's range of products, pack sizes and variants affects its physical availability. More variants can mean more relevance to different buying situations and more shelf presence, but they also bring complexity, cost and the risk of cannibalization. Sharp's framework provides a way to think about this trade off in terms of reach and availability rather than abstract positioning.

The duplication of purchase law suggests that most new variants will draw buyers from the brand's existing products and from large competitors, in proportion to their penetration, unless the variant enters a functional partition the brand does not currently serve. This implies that range extensions are most valuable when they open new buying situations or channels, for instance a single serve pack that allows a brand to be sold in convenience channels, a format that suits a different usage occasion, or a price point that suits a different retail environment. Variants that merely add flavours or claims within the same partition may add complexity without much growth.

From a manufacturing standpoint, every additional stock keeping unit adds changeovers, raw material and packaging complexity, forecasting difficulty and inventory. Many consumer goods companies have gone through cycles of range proliferation followed by rationalization. Sharp's framework helps to decide which variants to keep: those that materially increase the brand's reach and physical availability, particularly among light buyers and in new channels, are worth their complexity; those that merely duplicate existing coverage may not be. Rationalization based purely on unit sales or cost, without considering the variant's role in availability, risks cutting formats that are essential for reaching particular buyers.

The book's reasoning also applies to decisions about multiple brands within a portfolio. Because competing brands share customers in proportion to their size, a company that owns several brands in the same category will find that they largely share buyers with each other, as well as with competitors. Portfolio strategy therefore cannot rely on the idea that each brand serves a separate, distinct segment. The value of multiple brands lies more in occupying more shelf space, covering more partitions and price tiers, and spreading risk than in reaching completely different people.

How Brands Grow Part 2 extended these ideas into durables and services, where the manufacturing implications differ. For cars, appliances and electronics, purchase cycles are long, so a brand's mental availability must be maintained over years between purchases, and physical availability depends on dealer networks, retail partnerships, online configurators and the availability of models and options when buyers are ready. For services, physical availability includes opening hours, branch and ATM networks, call centres, digital channels and the ease of signing up. In each case, the operational capacity to serve buyers when and where they want to buy is a central driver of market share.

Finally, the laws have implications for production capacity planning in growth scenarios. If a brand pursues growth through broader distribution and reach, as Sharp recommends, volume growth will typically come in steps as new channels and outlets are added, rather than as a gradual increase in consumption among existing buyers. Manufacturing and logistics capacity should be planned in line with distribution gains, and supply chains should be designed to serve a wider, more fragmented set of outlets, which often means higher service costs per unit that must be weighed against the market share gains they enable.

## Criticism

How Brands Grow has been widely praised for its empirical grounding, but it has also attracted serious criticism, much of it focused on what its laws can and cannot explain. The first criticism is that the laws are descriptive rather than causal. Double jeopardy and duplication of purchase describe how markets look at a given moment, in relatively stable conditions, but they do not by themselves explain why some brands become large and others remain small. A brand's market share is the input to the Dirichlet model, not its output. Critics argue that the model can predict loyalty from market share very well, but it cannot tell a manager how to change market share in the first place, beyond the general advice to build mental and physical availability.

A second criticism concerns the role of differentiation and innovation. Many marketers and academics argue that Sharp underestimates the importance of meaningful product differences, particularly in categories driven by innovation, technology or design, and during periods of disruption. New categories and disruptive products often grow precisely because they are different in ways that matter to buyers. Once a market stabilizes, the Ehrenberg regularities reappear, but the process that determined which brands became large may well have been driven by differentiation. Seen in this light, the debate with writers such as Seth Godin is less a contradiction than a difference in which phase of a market's life each author describes.

A third line of criticism concerns the tone and certainty of the book. Sharp's style is polemical, and his description of findings as laws struck some readers as overstated, given that the regularities are statistical tendencies with exceptions rather than physical laws. Critics also noted that much of the underlying data comes from repeat purchase consumer goods categories in developed markets, and questioned how far the conclusions generalize to luxury goods, business to business markets, very new categories or highly personalized services. How Brands Grow Part 2 attempted to address some of these concerns by extending the evidence to more categories and regions.

A fourth criticism comes from advocates of targeting and personalization. They argue that digital media allow precise targeting at reasonable cost, and that Sharp's emphasis on broad reach ignores the efficiency gains available from reaching the most responsive audiences. Defenders of the book counter that narrow targeting often reaches people who would have bought anyway and misses the light buyers who drive growth, and the debate remains active.

From an operations perspective, one might also criticize the book for treating physical availability somewhat broadly, without engaging deeply with its costs. Expanding distribution to more outlets, offering more formats and maintaining high service levels all cost money, and there are diminishing returns. A manufacturer serving thousands of tiny outlets may find that the cost of service exceeds the margin earned. The book makes a powerful case that availability drives share, but leaves the detailed economics of availability, the optimization of network design, cost to serve and service level targets, to supply chain professionals.

None of these criticisms has displaced the book's central contribution. Even its critics generally accept the empirical regularities it describes. The disagreements concern interpretation, emphasis and what managers should do with the knowledge, which is a sign of how thoroughly the book reshaped the terms of the debate.

## Impact on Industry and Society

The impact of How Brands Grow on the marketing industry has been considerable and has grown over time rather than fading. It helped to establish evidence based marketing as a recognizable movement, in which practitioners are expected to justify their strategies with data on buyer behaviour and advertising effectiveness rather than with intuition or fashionable theory. The vocabulary of the book, including mental and physical availability, distinctive assets, light buyers, double jeopardy and category entry points, became common in agencies, brand teams and marketing education around the world.

The book's influence is visible in the strategies of some of the largest consumer goods companies. Mars has publicly worked with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute and has described its own marketing principles in terms closely aligned with Sharp's ideas, emphasizing penetration, reach and distinctive assets. In 2016, Procter and Gamble's chief brand officer Marc Pritchard indicated that the company had targeted its digital advertising too narrowly and was shifting toward broader reach, a move widely interpreted as consistent with Sharp's arguments. Many other companies sponsor the Institute's research, and its findings are routinely cited in marketing strategy documents and conference presentations.

The book also had a strong influence on commentary and education. Marketing columnists and teachers, among them the outspoken British commentator Mark Ritson, promoted its findings, and it became a standard reference in courses on brand management. Its influence was strengthened by the parallel work of Les Binet and Peter Field, and together these bodies of evidence shaped how many organizations allocate budgets between brand building and short term activation.

For manufacturing and supply chain management, the book's most important legacy is the elevation of physical availability to strategic status. By arguing that distribution, availability and retail execution are among the most powerful drivers of brand growth, Sharp gave supply chain and sales teams a stronger voice in commercial strategy. Concepts such as on shelf availability, route to market design, omnichannel coverage and pack format strategy came to be discussed as brand growth levers rather than purely as operational efficiency topics. This aligned with broader trends in consumer goods, including collaborative planning with retailers, investment in retail execution technology and the rise of e-commerce, where the digital shelf became a new arena for physical availability.

The book also influenced the debate about price promotions and their supply chain effects. By showing that most promotions do not build brands, it reinforced efforts by some manufacturers and retailers to reduce promotional intensity, adopt more stable pricing and reduce the demand volatility that promotions create. Although promotional activity remains widespread, the evidence presented in How Brands Grow gives supply chain leaders an argument they can use with commercial colleagues.

At a societal level, the influence of the book is more indirect. Its emphasis on broad reach and mass marketing runs counter to the trend toward hyper targeted, data intensive advertising, and some observers see in it a more transparent and less intrusive model of marketing, one that relies less on tracking individual behaviour. On the other hand, its focus on making brands easy to buy everywhere can be seen as reinforcing consumption and the dominance of large brands with the resources to achieve wide distribution and continuous advertising. Like any influential business book, its effects on society depend on how its insights are applied.

The book has also influenced how the sustainability transition is discussed in consumer goods. As companies redesign packaging to reduce plastic or switch to refill and reuse systems, the concepts of distinctive assets and physical availability help them assess the commercial risks. A packaging change that undermines recognition or a refill system available in only a few outlets may reduce a brand's mental and physical availability. Sharp's framework encourages companies to manage such transitions carefully, preserving distinctive cues and ensuring wide availability of new formats.

## Conclusion

How Brands Grow is an attempt to give marketing a scientific foundation, and in large measure it succeeds. Drawing on decades of research by Andrew Ehrenberg, Gerald Goodhardt, Byron Sharp and their colleagues, it shows that buying behaviour follows a small number of regular patterns. Brands differ mainly in how many people buy them, not in how loyal those people are. All brands lose customers and share them with competitors in predictable ways. Heavy buyers matter less, and light buyers more, than conventional wisdom assumed. And brands grow by becoming easier to think of and easier to buy for more people in more situations.

Read from the perspective of manufacturing and logistics, the book's most important message is that the supply chain is part of the brand. Physical availability, the ease with which a brand can be found and bought, depends on distribution coverage, pack formats, shelf presence, stock availability and retail execution. These are operational achievements, built by factories, warehouses, transport networks, sales teams and retail partners. Sharp's work gives them strategic weight, showing that they can matter as much as advertising in determining a brand's market share. The book also casts a critical light on practices such as heavy price promotions, which create costly volatility in supply chains while doing little to build brands, and it encourages careful management of packaging and product form as carriers of distinctive brand assets.

The book's limits are real. Its laws describe stable markets better than they explain how leadership is won, and it may understate the role of innovation and meaningful difference in the dynamic phases of a market. But even its critics accept that it has changed the conversation. For anyone who works in production, logistics or distribution, How Brands Grow offers a compelling reason to see their work not as a cost centre that supports marketing, but as one of the two pillars, alongside memory, on which brand growth rests.
