The Machine That Trusted Its Own Software
Between 1985 and 1987, a radiation therapy machine called the Therac-25 delivered massive overdoses of radiation to at least six patients across the United States and Canada, killing several of them and leaving others with severe, permanent injuries. The machine was meant to treat cancer by aiming a precisely controlled beam at a tumor. Instead, in a handful of documented cases, it delivered a beam roughly a hundred times stronger than intended, and almost nobody involved could explain how, since the machine itself reported that everything had gone perfectly.
Earlier versions of the same machine had relied on mechanical safety interlocks, physical hardware that made it impossible to fire a dangerous, unfiltered beam unless the equipment was correctly positioned. The Therac-25 removed most of these physical safeguards and placed that same responsibility entirely on software, trusting a computer program to enforce limits that earlier machines had enforced with metal and switches. This was, on its own, a reasonable engineering decision, since software checks are often faster and more flexible than physical ones. The problem was a flaw hidden deep inside that software, one that almost never appeared.
The flaw was a race condition, a kind of bug that only shows up when two things happen in an unusual order or at an unusual speed. If a skilled, fast-typing operator entered treatment settings and then corrected a mistake quickly enough, certain safety checks in the software could be skipped entirely, and the machine could switch into its high-power mode without properly configuring the beam. This required a very specific sequence of keystrokes, typed within a narrow window of a few seconds, which explains why the machine passed all standard testing and worked correctly the vast majority of the time.
When the malfunction did occur, the machine's response made things worse rather than better. Instead of clearly warning the operator, it displayed a cryptic message, often just the words malfunction and a number, giving no indication of the danger and continuing to permit the operator to proceed. Some patients reported a sensation like an intense electric shock and told the technicians something was deeply wrong, but with no dramatic alarm from the machine itself, those warnings were sometimes dismissed.
It took an unusually persistent engineer named Nancy Leveson, working with a colleague, to trace the failures back to their true cause, since the manufacturer initially insisted the machine could not possibly be at fault. Her investigation became one of the most influential case studies in the history of software safety, taught for decades afterward to engineers building systems where a single unexamined line of code can cost a human life. The lesson was not that computers are inherently dangerous, but that removing a physical safeguard requires replacing it with something just as trustworthy, tested with the same seriousness as the hardware it replaces.
